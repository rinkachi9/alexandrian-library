# Lucid dreaming

## Summary

A lucid dream is a dream in which the dreamer knows that they are dreaming. It occurs mostly in REM sleep and has been verified in the laboratory with pre-arranged eye signals. Surveys suggest that many people have had at least one lucid dream, while frequent lucid dreaming is rare. Techniques exist to raise the chance, with modest evidence. The state is studied for what it says about consciousness and is also tried for nightmares, with limited evidence.

*This note is educational. It is not medical advice.*

## Core concepts

### Definition

*Lucid dreaming* is a state in which a person is aware during a dream that it is a dream, and may be able to influence its content. Voss and colleagues (2009) found in a sleep laboratory that lucid dreaming has features of both waking and ordinary dreaming. Baird, Mota-Rolim and Dresler (2019) review the neuroscience: lucidity during REM sleep is linked to higher activity in frontal and parietal areas that are usually less active during REM sleep.

### Analogy

An ordinary dream is like watching a film while believing it is real life. A lucid dream is the moment when the viewer realizes it is a film and can look around the cinema. The limit of the analogy is that the dreamer remains inside the dream, and has only partial control.

### Levels and induction

- **Verification.** Researchers confirm lucid dreams by asking trained dreamers to make a pre-arranged sequence of eye movements, which appear on the recording during REM sleep.
- **Induction techniques.** Common methods are reality checks during the day, keeping a dream journal, mnemonic induction (intending to recognize a dream when it occurs) and waking up after several hours of sleep and going back to sleep. Stumbrys and colleagues (2012) reviewed the evidence in a systematic review: some techniques, especially mnemonic ones, show promise, and the studies are small and of mixed quality.

## Worked example

A person who wants to try lucid dreaming follows a one-month plan.

| Week | Practice |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Write down dreams each morning |
| 2 | Add 5 reality checks per day, for example asking "am I dreaming?" and reading text twice |
| 3 | Before sleep, repeat the intention to notice a dream |
| 4 | Continue, and note how many dreams were recalled and how many were lucid |

Success is measured by counts in the journal, and not by a feeling. Results vary, and many people have none in a month.

## Trade offs and when to use it

Possible benefits are curiosity and practice of attention. Claims about therapy for nightmares have some support, but the evidence is limited. Risks: interrupted sleep with some techniques, and confusion between dreaming and waking for some people. Anyone with a sleep disorder or a mental health condition should talk to a clinician before trying induction.

## Common mistakes

- **Expecting full control.** Control is partial and varies.
- **Sacrificing sleep.** Waking techniques cost sleep, and sleep matters more.
- **Treating dream content as prediction.** There is no evidence that it predicts anything.

2. In which sleep stage does it mostly occur?
