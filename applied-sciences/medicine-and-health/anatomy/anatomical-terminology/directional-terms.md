# Directional terms

## Summary

Directional terms describe where a structure lies relative to another, always assuming the anatomical position. They come in opposite pairs: superior and inferior, anterior and posterior, medial and lateral, proximal and distal, superficial and deep, internal and external, central and peripheral, and ipsilateral and contralateral. Medicine uses Latin-based terms worldwide, which avoids misunderstandings about which part of the body is meant.

## Prerequisites

- [Anatomical position](anatomical-position.md)

## Core concepts

### Definition

A *directional term* states the position of one structure relative to another in the anatomical position. The terms are relative: the knee is proximal to the ankle but distal to the hip.

### Analogy

Directional terms work like relative directions in a building ("the office is upstairs and to the left of the stairs"). Without a reference point, "upstairs" means nothing. In anatomy the reference is the anatomical position plus a named structure. The analogy breaks because in a building "left" depends on where you stand, while "lateral" in anatomy always means away from the body's midline.

### Mechanism

| Term (Latin) | Meaning | Opposite | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Superior (*superior*), also cranial | Toward the head | Inferior (*inferior*), caudal | The head is superior to the neck |
| Anterior (*anterior*), also ventral | Toward the front | Posterior (*posterior*), dorsal | The sternum is anterior to the heart |
| Medial (*medialis*) | Toward the midline | Lateral (*lateralis*) | The little finger is medial to the thumb |
| Proximal (*proximalis*) | Closer to the trunk or the point of origin (limbs) | Distal (*distalis*) | The elbow is proximal to the wrist |
| Superficial (*superficialis*) | Closer to the body surface | Deep (*profundus*) | The skin is superficial to the muscles |
| Internal (*internus*) | Inside a cavity or structure | External (*externus*) | The internal oblique lies inside the external oblique |
| Central (*centralis*) | Toward the center | Peripheral (*peripheralis*) | The brain is part of the central nervous system |
| Palmar (*palmaris*) | On the palm side of the hand | Dorsal (*dorsalis*) | The fingernails are on the dorsal side |
| Plantar (*plantaris*) | On the sole of the foot | Dorsal | The arch is on the plantar side |
| Ipsilateral | On the same side | Contralateral, on the opposite side | The left eye is ipsilateral to the left ear and contralateral to the right eye |

Some terms are specific to body parts: in the forearm, *radial* means the thumb side and *ulnar* the little finger side; in the leg, *tibial* and *fibular*.

## Worked example

A note reads: "a 2 cm lesion on the dorsal surface of the left hand, proximal to the third knuckle, ulnar to the second metacarpal". Translated: on the back of the left hand, closer to the wrist than the middle finger knuckle, on the little finger side of the bone that leads to the index finger. Each term narrows the location, and anyone trained in anatomy finds the same spot.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- Some terms overlap: in humans, anterior and ventral mean the same, but in four-legged animals ventral means the belly side, which points down. Veterinary and human anatomy differ for this reason.
- "Dorsal" means the back of the body and of the hand, but the top of the foot.

## Common mistakes

- **Using proximal and distal for the trunk.** These terms are for limbs and branching structures; use superior and inferior for the trunk.
- **Mixing internal and medial.** Internal refers to depth within a cavity; medial refers to closeness to the midline.

## Practice

1. Is the wrist proximal or distal to the elbow?
2. Which side of the forearm is the radial side?
3. Is the right lung ipsilateral or contralateral to the left kidney?

### Answers

1. Distal.
2. The thumb side, which is lateral.
3. Contralateral.
