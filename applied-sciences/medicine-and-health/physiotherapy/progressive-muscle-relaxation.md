# Progressive muscle relaxation (Jacobson)

## Summary

Progressive muscle relaxation was developed by the American physician and psychiatrist Edmund Jacobson in the 1920s and 1930s. He observed that reducing muscle tension calms the nervous system, reduces anxiety and helps the body quiet down. The method is simple: tense a muscle group while breathing in, hold for about 5 seconds, then relax it while breathing out, and notice the difference. Meta-analyses show it reduces anxiety.

*This note is educational. It is not medical advice and does not replace a doctor, dietitian or pharmacist, especially during pregnancy, illness or when taking medicines.*

No prior notes are needed.

## Core concepts

### Definition

*Progressive muscle relaxation* (PMR) is a relaxation technique in which muscle groups are deliberately tensed and then relaxed in a fixed order, to learn to recognize and release tension.

### Analogy

PMR is like learning what silence sounds like by first turning the music up. The contrast between loud and quiet makes the quiet easier to notice and find again. The analogy breaks because the goal is not contrast for its own sake, but learning to relax without the tensing step.

### Mechanism

Tensing a muscle and then releasing it makes the sensation of relaxation more noticeable. With practice, a person recognizes tension earlier and can release it. Reduced muscle tension decreases sensory input to the brain and is associated with lower sympathetic activity.

**Basic rules.**

- Tense a muscle group with an in-breath, hold for about 5 seconds, then relax with an out-breath.
- Pause for a dozen or so seconds to observe the body's response and feel the difference between tension and relaxation.
- Practice lying down, which favors relaxation, or half-lying or sitting; avoid discomfort, for example in the spine.

**Sequence.**

1. **Feet.** Focus on the right or left foot, pull the toes toward the knee, tense, wait 5 seconds and relax. Repeat with the other foot. Curl the toes toward the sole and feel the difference before and after.
2. **Legs and trunk.** Tense and relax the calves, thighs, whole legs, buttocks, and abdomen (press it toward the spine). Tense the chest: take a deep breath, hold briefly and slowly let the air out.
3. **Hands.** Clench the fists, hold and relax.
4. **Arms and back.** Forearms (you can press the elbow into the floor), upper arms, shoulders, upper back (pull the shoulder blades together, tense, wait, relax), neck (tilt the head back, then forward).
5. **Face.** Squeeze the eyelids, wrinkle the forehead, purse the lips, press the tongue against the palate. The aim is always to feel the difference between tension and relaxation.

### Claimed effects and evidence

Each entry states the claimed effect and how strong the evidence is, using the grades defined in the [Evidence grades](../nutrition/evidence-grades.md) note.

- **Reduced anxiety.** Meta-analyses show moderate effects. Evidence: moderate.
- **Better sleep.** Some trials show improvements in insomnia. Evidence: limited.
- **Lower blood pressure.** Small effects in some trials. Evidence: limited.

## Worked example

A person with trouble falling asleep practices PMR in bed for 15 minutes each night for 3 weeks, following the sequence from feet to face. After the first week, they shorten the routine by combining muscle groups. Their sleep latency (see [Sleep latency](../physiology/sleep/sleep-latency.md)) drops from about 40 to 25 minutes.

## Trade offs and when to use it

PMR is easy to learn and safe. People with injuries or high blood pressure should tense gently and avoid holding the breath for long.

## Common mistakes

- **Tensing too hard and causing cramps.** Use moderate tension.
- **Rushing through the sequence.** Pause to notice the relaxation after each group.

## Practice

1. Who developed progressive muscle relaxation?
2. How long is each muscle group tensed?
3. Which position is best for practice?

### Answers

1. Edmund Jacobson.
2. About 5 seconds.
3. Lying down, or half-lying or sitting without discomfort.
