# Chromium

## Summary

Chromium (trivalent chromium) is a trace element thought to enhance the action of insulin, although whether it is truly essential is debated: in 2014 EFSA concluded there is no evidence of essentiality. Supplements such as chromium picolinate are marketed for blood glucose control and weight loss. Meta-analyses show at most small effects on blood glucose in type 2 diabetes and negligible effects on weight.

*This note is educational. It is not medical advice and does not replace a doctor, dietitian or pharmacist, especially during pregnancy, illness or when taking medicines.*

## Core concepts

### Definition

*Chromium* (Cr) is a metal that exists in several forms. *Trivalent chromium* (Cr3+) occurs in food and supplements; *hexavalent chromium* (Cr6+) is an industrial toxin and carcinogen. *Chromium picolinate* is a common supplement form.

### Analogy

Chromium was described as a key that helps insulin open the door for glucose into cells. Better data suggest it is at most a small helper holding the door, and many people manage fine without noticing it. The analogy breaks because the exact molecular role is still unknown.

### Mechanism

An oligopeptide called chromodulin was proposed to bind chromium and amplify insulin receptor signaling. Severe insulin resistance reversed by chromium was reported in patients on long-term intravenous nutrition without chromium. In people eating normal diets, no clear deficiency state has been defined. In 2014 the EFSA panel concluded there is no evidence that chromium is essential and set no reference value.

| Item | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Adequate intake (US) | 35 µg men, 25 µg women (EFSA: no reference value) |
| UL | Not set |
| Food sources | Meat (beef, chicken), fish (tuna, salmon), whole grains (brown rice, oats), nuts (almonds, hazelnuts), seeds (sunflower, sesame), vegetables (broccoli, potatoes), black pepper, fortified cereals |

### Proposed effects and evidence

Each entry states the proposed effect and how strong the human evidence is, using the grades defined in the [Evidence grades](../evidence-grades.md) note.

- **Blood glucose regulation.** Small reductions in fasting glucose in some meta-analyses of type 2 diabetes; results inconsistent. Evidence: limited.
- **Carbohydrate metabolism and glycogen synthesis.** Proposed role; essentiality debated. Evidence: limited.
- **Fat and protein metabolism.** No clear evidence. Evidence: very limited.
- **Heart health through insulin sensitivity.** No outcome trials. Evidence: very limited.
- **Weight loss and reduced sugar cravings.** Meta-analysis showed about 0.5 kg difference, not clinically meaningful. Evidence: not supported.
- **Nervous system function.** Evidence is limited. Evidence: very limited.
- **Immune function.** No evidence. Evidence: very limited.
- **Cholesterol regulation.** Mixed results. Evidence: limited.
- **Antioxidant protection.** Role not established. Evidence: very limited.
- **Skin health and acne.** No evidence. Evidence: very limited.

## Worked example

A person with type 2 diabetes takes 200 µg chromium picolinate daily for 3 months. In a review by Costello and colleagues (2016), most trials showed small or no improvements in HbA1c, and the effect was not judged clinically meaningful. Standard diabetes treatment and lifestyle changes have much larger effects.

## Trade offs and when to use it

Chromium supplements appear safe at usual doses but offer little benefit; they should not replace diabetes treatment.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying chromium for weight loss.** Effects are negligible.
- **Confusing trivalent and hexavalent chromium.** Only Cr3+ occurs in food; Cr6+ is an industrial toxin.

## Practice

1. Which form of chromium is in food?
2. What did EFSA conclude in 2014?
3. What is the effect of chromium on weight?

### Answers

1. Trivalent chromium.
2. There is no evidence that chromium is essential.
3. Negligible.
