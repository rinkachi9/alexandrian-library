# Bisphenol A

## Summary

Bisphenol A (BPA) is a building block of polycarbonate plastics and epoxy resins that line food cans. It mimics estrogen weakly and migrates into food, especially with heat. In 2023, EFSA lowered the tolerable daily intake by about 20,000 times, to 0.2 nanograms per kilogram of body weight, concluding that dietary exposure exceeds safe levels for much of the population, mainly because of immune effects in animals. The EU banned BPA in baby bottles (2011) and decided in 2024 to ban it in food contact materials.

## Core concepts

### Definition

*Bisphenol A* is 4,4'-(propane-2,2-diyl)diphenol. *Tolerable daily intake* (TDI) is the amount that can be ingested daily over a lifetime without appreciable risk. *BPS* and *BPF* are related bisphenols used as substitutes.

### Analogy

BPA is like a molecule that speaks a faint version of the estrogen language. In small amounts, the body barely notices; the question is whether constant background chatter affects development and immunity. The analogy breaks because BPA acts on several receptors, not only estrogen.

### Mechanism

BPA binds estrogen receptors and the G protein-coupled estrogen receptor, and affects other pathways. It is metabolized quickly (half-life about 6 hours) but exposure is continuous. EFSA based its 2023 TDI on increases in T helper cells (an immune effect) in mice.

### Claimed risks and evidence

Each entry states the claimed risk and how strong the evidence for it is at typical exposures, using the grades in [Evidence grades](../nutrition/evidence-grades.md).

- **Endocrine disruption.** Established in laboratory and animal studies; human effects at typical exposure debated. Evidence: moderate.
- **Exposure exceeds EFSA safe levels.** EFSA 2023 concluded exposure exceeds the new TDI in all age groups with high consumption. Evidence: moderate.
- **BPA-free substitutes are safe.** BPS and BPF show similar activity in laboratory studies. Evidence: limited.

## Worked example

Canned soups and drinks are significant BPA sources. In a small study, people who ate one serving of canned soup daily for 5 days had urinary BPA levels over 1,000 percent higher than when they ate fresh soup. Choosing fresh or frozen foods and glass containers reduces exposure.

## Trade offs and when to use it

Reducing BPA exposure is reasonable, especially in pregnancy and for infants. Choosing "BPA-free" alone may not avoid similar bisphenols.

## Common mistakes

- **Heating food in polycarbonate.** Heat increases migration.
- **Assuming BPA-free means bisphenol-free.** Substitutes may be similar.
