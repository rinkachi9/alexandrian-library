# Repetition ranges and load

## Summary

A repetition range sets how many repetitions each set should contain, and the load is chosen so the set is hard within that range. The classic hypertrophy range is 8 to 12 repetitions: if fewer than 8 are possible, the load is too heavy; if more than 12, too light; after reaching 12, the load is increased. Research shows that muscle growth is similar across a wide range of about 6 to 30 or more repetitions when sets are taken close to failure, while heavy loads (1 to 5 repetitions) remain best for maximal strength.

## Core concepts

### Definition

A *repetition range* is the target number of repetitions per set. *Load* (intensity) is the weight lifted, often expressed as %1RM. *Double progression* is a method in which repetitions are increased within a range and the load is raised once the top of the range is reached.

### Analogy

Choosing a rep range is like choosing a gear on a bicycle. Low gears (light loads, many repetitions) and high gears (heavy loads, few repetitions) can both get you up the hill (stimulate growth) if you pedal hard enough; the high gear builds more maximal force. The analogy breaks because muscles also adapt specifically: lifting heavy improves the skill of lifting heavy.

### Mechanism

**The 8 to 12 rule.** The range of 8 to 12 means at least 8 and at most 12 repetitions. If 8 are not possible, the weight is too heavy; if more than 12 are possible, it is too light. After completing 12 repetitions with a weight, increase the load in the next set or session. For example, after bench pressing 40 kg for 12 repetitions, increase to 45 kg and perform 8 or 9 repetitions.

**What research shows.**

- Hypertrophy: similar for low (about 30 percent 1RM) and high (above 60 percent) loads when sets go close to failure (Schoenfeld and colleagues, 2017).
- Strength: greater with heavy loads, because of specificity.
- Very light loads require high repetitions and discomfort to reach failure.

**Typical ranges by goal (guidelines).**

| Goal | Repetitions | %1RM | Rest |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Maximal strength | 1 to 5 | 85 to 100 | 3 to 5 min |
| Hypertrophy | 6 to 20 (often 8 to 12) | 60 to 85 | 1 to 3 min |
| Muscular endurance | 15 or more | 45 to 65 | Under 1 min |

## Worked example

A beginner leg presses 100 kg for 10, 9 and 8 repetitions. Each week they add repetitions until all sets reach 12; then they add 10 kg and start again near 8. Over 12 weeks, the load rises from 100 kg to about 130 kg. This is progressive overload in its simplest form ([Progressive overload](../intensification-methods/progressive-overload.md)).

## Trade offs and when to use it

- Moderate ranges (6 to 15) balance mechanical tension, fatigue and time.
- Heavy sets need longer rests and more warm-up; very light sets cause more discomfort and fatigue per set.

## Common mistakes

- **Stopping at 12 repetitions when 20 were possible.** The load must make the set hard.
- **Believing only 8 to 12 builds muscle.** A broad range works when effort is high.

## Practice

1. What should happen when 12 repetitions are achieved with a load?
2. Which repetition range is best for maximal strength?
3. What makes light loads effective for hypertrophy?

### Answers

1. Increase the load in the next set or session.
2. About 1 to 5 repetitions at 85 to 100 percent of 1RM.
3. Taking sets close to failure, which recruits high-threshold motor units.
