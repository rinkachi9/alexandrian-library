# Career transitions

## Summary

A career transition is a change in role, employer, field or working life, planned or forced. Schlossberg's model says that how well a person handles a transition depends on the nature of the change, the surroundings before and after it, and the person. The protean career idea adds that the person, not the organization, manages the path. The practical approach is to treat a change as a series of small, reversible experiments with support in place.

## Prerequisites

[Career planning process](career-planning-process.md).

## Core concepts

### Transition

*Definition.* Schlossberg (1981) defined a transition as an event or a non-event that results in a change in assumptions about oneself and the world, and so requires a change in behavior and relationships. A *non-event* is an expected change that does not happen, such as a promotion that never comes.

*Mechanism.* The 1981 model looks at the characteristics of the transition (for example its timing and whether it was chosen), the person's environment before and after, and the person. Later descriptions group the factors as the "4 S": situation, self, support and strategies. TODO: confirm the 4 S formulation in a primary Schlossberg source.

*Analogy.* A transition is like crossing a river. The width and current are the situation, your swimming skill is the self, a rope from the bank is the support and your chosen route is the strategy. The analogy fails because many transitions have no clear far bank.

### The protean career

*Definition.* Hall (1996) described the protean career as one driven by the person's own values and managed by the person rather than the organization. The name refers to Proteus, who changed shape.

*Boundaries.* The idea fits people with options. For those with few, "manage your own career" can be an extra burden. This is an interpretation.

### Experiments over leaps

Planned happenstance (Mitchell et al., 1999) suggests that exploring through small actions produces chances. In a transition, small tests reduce the cost of being wrong.

## Worked example

Situation: Marek, an accountant, thinks about moving to financial analysis in another company. The details are invented.

| Factor | Question | Marek's answer | Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Situation | Chosen? Timing? Duration? | Chosen, no deadline | Set a six-month window |
| Self | Strengths, beliefs, finances? | Strong Excel, no modeling experience, six months of savings | Build one model on public data |
| Support | Who helps? | A former colleague works as an analyst | Ask for a 30-minute talk |
| Strategies | What will I try? | Stay in role, test the field | Do a small internal analysis project |

After six months, he reviews: did the experiment increase interest and confidence? If yes, apply. If not, the cost was low.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- Experiments take time. If a transition is forced (layoff), shorten the cycle and apply in parallel.
- Support from other people has a large effect on how a transition feels, but no source in this note quantifies it.
- A full jump can be right when the old role is harmful. In that case, secure a financial buffer first.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the start date at the new job as the end of the transition. Adjustment continues (see [first 90 days](../growth/first-90-days.md)).
- Skipping the support question. Choose a person, not a vague "network".
- Deciding based on the worst day at the current job. Compare against the actual new role.
