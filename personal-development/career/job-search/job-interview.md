# Job interview

## Summary

A job interview is a conversation in which an employer estimates how well you will do the job and you estimate whether you want it. Research on personnel selection finds that structured interviews, where every candidate gets the same questions and is rated on the same scale, predict job performance better than unstructured ones. For the candidate this means preparing evidence for questions about past behavior, and preparing your own questions, because the end of the interview is the point where you can steer the conversation.

## Core concepts

### Structured and unstructured interviews

*Definition.* In a *structured interview*, questions are fixed in advance, each candidate gets the same ones and answers are scored against a rating scale. In an *unstructured interview*, the interviewer improvises.

*Evidence.* In Schmidt and Hunter's 1998 review of 19 selection procedures, the combination of a general mental ability test and a structured interview was among the three with the highest multivariate validity for job performance (.63). In 2022, Sackett, Zhang, Berry and Lievens argued that earlier estimates were inflated by overcorrection for range restriction. Their revised estimates were lower by .10 to .20, but structured interviews emerged as the top-ranked selection procedure. A narrative and quantitative review of structured interviews was published in 2014 (Levashina et al.).

*Analogy.* A structured interview is like a standard exam: every student faces the same questions and marking guide. It breaks down because an interview is also a social event, and the candidate evaluates the employer too.

*Boundaries.* Validity coefficients describe average predictions across many people. They say little about one interview. The authors of the 2022 paper also warn against preferring one predictor everywhere.

### Question types

- **Behavioral:** "Tell me about a time when you..." asks about past behavior.
- **Situational:** "What would you do if..." asks about intended behavior.

### Questions you ask

The source notes of this library record two questions for the end of the interview:

1. "What is the biggest opportunity facing the department?"
2. "Six months from now, what would I have needed to accomplish to exceed your expectations?"

Why they work: the first tells you what the work is for and shows you think about the team's goals. The second tells you how success is judged, which you can use in your answers and in your first weeks (see [first 90 days](../growth/first-90-days.md)).

The source notes also advise that you let the interviewer see you write down the answer, to show you will be part of the solution. This is practitioner advice. No research is cited for it, so treat it as an opinion.

## Worked example

Question: "Tell me about a time you handled a disagreement in a team." (The answer is invented.)

- **Situation:** In a migration project, two colleagues disagreed on the order of tasks.
- **Task:** As coordinator, I had to restore agreement without delaying the deadline.
- **Action:** I asked each person for the risk they feared, listed both on a shared page and proposed a pilot of one module.
- **Result:** The pilot finished in two days, the team chose a hybrid order, and we delivered one day early.

Check the answer: it names a concrete case, shows your own action and gives a result. Practice with a timer; two minutes per answer is a common target, and it is a rule of thumb.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- Prepared answers can sound rehearsed. Prepare stories, not scripts.
- Employers vary. Many interviews are less structured than the research ideal, so be ready to supply structure yourself by giving examples.
- Asking about pay too early can change the tone. See [salary and offer negotiation](salary-and-offer-negotiation.md).

## Common mistakes

- Answering with "we" for everything. Fix: say what you did.
- Not asking any question. Fix: prepare three and choose the best during the talk.
- Speaking badly about a former employer. Fix: describe what you learned.
