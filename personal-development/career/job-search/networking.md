# Networking

## Summary

Networking means building, maintaining and using professional relationships. Two lines of research support it. Granovetter's "strength of weak ties" argued that acquaintances give access to information that close friends already share with you. A longitudinal study found that networking was related to salary and to how fast salary grew, and to career satisfaction at a given time. The practical rule is to give value, ask specific questions and keep in touch before you need anything.

## Core concepts

### Weak ties

*Definition.* In a social network, a *strong tie* is a close, frequent relationship, and a *weak tie* is an acquaintance. Granovetter (1973) argued that the overlap between two people's friendship circles grows with the strength of their tie, so weak ties link otherwise separate groups and carry new information.

*Analogy.* Think of a city's neighborhoods. Close friends live in the same one and know the same shops. An acquaintance who lives across town knows shops you have never heard of. The analogy breaks because information is not the only thing a tie gives: close ties offer trust and support.

*Mechanism.* A job lead has to pass through a person. Your close contacts have mostly heard the same news as you. A weak tie belongs to another group and may hear of openings you cannot see.

*Boundaries.* The 1973 paper is a theoretical argument with an empirical base from its time. Do not read it as "only weak ties help".

### What networking predicts

Wolff and Moser (2009) defined networking as building, maintaining and using relationships. They measured it in six facets and tracked salary and career satisfaction for three years. Networking was related to concurrent salary and to the growth rate of salary over time, and to concurrent career satisfaction. ### Methods

- **Maintain:** short messages after a talk, sharing a useful article, congratulating a change.
- **Build:** attend events where you can meet people from other groups.
- **Use:** ask for information, an introduction or a referral, and be specific.

## Worked example

Goal: learn what a product analyst does day to day (invented scenario). Steps:

1. Find a person two steps away, for example a former classmate's colleague.
2. Ask the shared contact for an introduction in one sentence.
3. Send a message: "Hello, I am exploring product analysis. Could I ask you three questions in a 20-minute call? I have prepared them in advance."
4. In the call, ask about a normal week, the hardest part and how the person entered the field.
5. Send thanks the same day, and later let them know what you did with the advice.

Target: two such conversations per week for six weeks, which is twelve conversations. Track them in a list with date, person and one thing learned.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- Networking takes time and rarely pays back quickly.
- It can feel awkward. A fixed routine and specific questions reduce the pressure.
- Some environments rely on referrals more than others. Check how hiring works in your field.

## Common mistakes

- Asking for a job in the first message. Fix: ask for information and advice.
- Networking only during a job search. Fix: keep a light routine all year.
- Taking without giving. Fix: share a resource or an introduction in return.
