# Salary and offer negotiation

## Summary

Negotiation is a process in which two parties with partly shared and partly conflicting interests try to agree on terms. The research most useful for an offer discussion is on anchoring: the first number mentioned strongly pulls the final settlement, but the pull weakens when the other side concentrates on its own alternatives and targets. Good preparation means knowing the market range, your walk-away point, what else matters to you besides pay and what the employer cares about.

## Prerequisites

[Job interview](job-interview.md). Know your values from [interests, values and strengths](../self-assessment/interests-values-and-strengths.md).

## Core concepts

### Terms

- **Interest:** why you want something (security, recognition, learning). A *position* is the specific demand ("80,000 per year"). The "principled negotiation" approach of Fisher and Ury asks you to focus on interests behind positions and to look for options that serve both sides.
- **BATNA:** best alternative to a negotiated agreement. It is what you will do if no deal happens. A strong BATNA gives you the freedom to say no.
- **Reservation point:** the worst terms you would still accept.
- **Target:** the outcome you aim for.
- **Anchor:** the first offer or number, which sets a reference.

### Anchoring evidence

In three experiments, whichever party made the first offer, buyer or seller, obtained a better outcome, and first offers were a strong predictor of final settlement prices. The advantage disappeared when the party who did not make the first offer focused on information inconsistent with it: the opponent's alternatives, the opponent's reservation price or one's own target. The effects held in face-to-face and e-mail negotiations (Galinsky and Mussweiler, 2001).

*Analogy.* An anchor works like a fence post in a field: all later bargaining happens around it. The analogy fails in that you can move the post by changing what you pay attention to.

*Mechanism.* People adjust from a number they hear and adjust too little. Thinking about the other side's alternatives or your own target replaces the anchor with a different reference.

*Boundaries.* These were experiments with defined price tasks, and a salary discussion has more issues. The finding does not say that you must always name the first number, and it depends on how well you know the range.

### Beyond base pay

Other terms may matter: bonus, start date, remote work, training budget, title, review date. They can create value for both sides because people value them differently.

## Worked example

Invented numbers in a currency of your choice. Research shows the market range for the role is 60,000 to 75,000 per year. Your BATNA is your current job at 62,000. Your reservation point is 64,000, because a move has costs. Your target is 72,000.

1. Prepare an anchor slightly above the target, for example 75,000, with reasons from the posting and your evidence.
2. The employer offers 66,000. This is within the range and above your reservation point.
3. You answer with interests: "I am excited about the role. Based on the scope and my results in X, I was looking for 72,000. What flexibility is there on base pay or on other terms?"
4. The employer offers 69,000 and an extra training budget. You compare to your target and BATNA and accept or ask for a short time to decide.

At each step, check that the offer is above your reservation point. If it is not, you walk away or look for other terms.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- Negotiating can raise pay but may harm the relationship if done aggressively. A collaborative tone protects both.
- If you do not know the market range, make research the first step before naming a number.

## Common mistakes

- Naming no number and hoping the employer is generous. Fix: have a range and reasons.
- Accepting at once. Fix: ask for time to review, in writing.
- Arguing from personal needs ("I have a loan"). Fix: argue from the value of the role and market data.
