# Labor market and work trends

## Summary

A career decision is made against a labor market: who wants which skills, where, and for how much. Public classifications such as O*NET (United States) and ESCO (European Union) describe occupations and skills in a standard vocabulary, so you can compare a job posting, a training course and your own skills in the same terms. Forecasts about the future of work are uncertain, so use them to ask questions, not to make a bet on one prediction.

## Prerequisites

None.

## Core concepts

### Vocabulary

- **Labor market.** The place where employers who need work done meet people who offer work. Demand is the number of positions employers want to fill. Supply is the number of people who want them.
- **Occupation.** A group of jobs with similar tasks, such as "data analyst". A *job* is a position at one employer. An *industry* is what the employer sells.
- **Skill.** An ability that can be learned and used in tasks. See [skills](../../skills/README.md).

### Classifications

*Definition.* An occupational classification is a shared dictionary of occupations and the skills they need.

- **O*NET.** A database from the U.S. Department of Labor that describes occupations by tasks, skills, knowledge and interests. It is linked to the O*NET Interest Profiler, which is based on Holland's six types, and to the occupation lists in O*NET OnLine (checked 2026-10).
- **ESCO.** The European Commission's multilingual classification of skills, competences and occupations. The ESCO site reports 3,039 occupations and 13,939 skills, linked to each other, in 28 languages (checked 2026-10).

*Analogy.* A classification is like a library catalog: the same book has one entry no matter who shelves it, so different people can find it. The limit is that a catalog changes slowly, while the real work changes faster than the entries.

*Mechanism.* Each occupation lists essential skills and optional skills. You compare that list with your own inventory, and the difference shows what to learn. See [skills inventory and gap analysis](../self-assessment/skills-inventory-and-gap-analysis.md).

### Reading a job posting

A posting mixes a real need with a wish list. Look for tasks stated with verbs and outputs ("builds weekly reports for sales") rather than adjectives ("dynamic"). Requirements that appear in many postings for the same occupation are more likely real.

## Worked example

Goal: compare two target occupations in a way you can repeat.

1. Choose two occupations from your shortlist. Look each up in O*NET OnLine or ESCO and copy the list of essential skills.
2. Collect five current postings for each from your region.
3. Count how many postings mention each skill.
4. Record the result in a table.

| Occupation | Skill | In classification? | Postings (of 5) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Occupation A | Skill 1 | yes | 5 |
| Occupation A | Skill 2 | yes | 2 |
| Occupation A | Skill 3 | no | 4 |

Skill 3 is not in the classification but appears in four of five postings. It is probably a current market need or a tool name. The numbers in this table are placeholders, and your own search will give different values.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- Classifications are broad and stable, so they are good for comparison and bad for the newest tools.
- Postings are current but biased toward employers who advertise publicly. Many jobs are filled through contacts (see [networking](../job-search/networking.md)).

## Common mistakes

- Choosing an occupation from a single forecast. Forecasts are projections with assumptions.
- Reading a posting as a fixed checklist. Employers often hire people who meet most requirements.
- Ignoring location. A classification describes an occupation in general, not the market where you live.
