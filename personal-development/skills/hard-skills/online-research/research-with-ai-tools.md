# Research with AI tools

## Summary

AI assistants based on large language models can speed up research: they explain unfamiliar topics, suggest search terms and sources, summarize documents and draft structures. They also produce fluent, confident text that can be wrong, including invented facts and citations. A 2023 study in Scientific Reports found that 55% of the references generated by GPT-3.5 and 18% of those generated by GPT-4 in short literature reviews did not exist. Newer tools that search the web and cite sources reduce but do not remove this problem. The safe pattern is to use AI for orientation and drafting, and to verify every factual claim and source in the original before relying on it.

## Core concepts

### Definition

An *AI research assistant* is a tool based on a large language model (LLM) that answers questions, summarizes or generates text, sometimes combined with web search or document retrieval. A *large language model* is a statistical model trained on large amounts of text to predict likely continuations. *Hallucination* (or *confabulation*) is the generation of content that is fluent but false or unsupported, such as invented facts, quotes or references.

*Analogy.* An AI assistant is like a very well-read colleague with an excellent memory for how things are usually said, who never says "I don't know" unless asked to. Their quick explanations save you hours, and their suggestions of where to look are often good. But when they are unsure, they still answer in the same confident tone, and occasionally they cite a book that does not exist. You would check that colleague's references before quoting them in a report. The analogy breaks because a human colleague can tell you how sure they are and why, while a model's confidence is not a reliable signal of accuracy.

### Why errors happen

- A language model generates text that is statistically plausible, not text checked against a database of facts.
- Citations are a common failure: the model produces a plausible author, title and journal combination.
- Knowledge has a cut-off date unless the tool searches the web.
- Tools that search the web can still misread, misattribute or overgeneralize what a page says.
- The way a question is phrased can push the answer toward agreement with the user.

### Evidence on fabricated citations

Walters and Wilder (2023) asked ChatGPT based on GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 to write short literature reviews on 42 topics and checked the 636 references. 55% of the GPT-3.5 references and 18% of the GPT-4 references were fabricated. Of the real references, 43% (GPT-3.5) and 24% (GPT-4) contained substantive errors. These rates apply to those model versions and that task. Later models and tools with web search perform differently, but the need to verify remains.

### Good uses

| Use | Why it helps | Check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Orientation in a new topic | Quick map of concepts and vocabulary | Confirm key facts in a reliable source |
| Search term generation | Synonyms, expert terms, other languages | Test them in real searches |
| Source suggestions | Names of reviews, organizations, standards | Find each source yourself |
| Summarizing documents you provide | Saves reading time | Compare the summary with the document for key points |
| Structuring and drafting | Outlines, first drafts, rewriting | Edit, verify claims, add your own analysis |
| Explaining a text or code | Faster understanding | Test the explanation on examples |
| Translation | Fast access to other languages | Check key terms, especially legal and technical |

### Risky uses

- Asking for statistics or quotes without verification.
- Asking for references and citing them unchecked.
- Legal, medical and financial conclusions without expert or primary-source checks.
- Recent events, prices, laws and rules that change.
- Uploading confidential or personal data to tools without checking data handling terms.

### A verification routine

1. **Ask for sources** for every factual claim, and treat the answer as a list of leads.
2. **Open every source.** Check that it exists and says what the summary claims. Use DOIs, library databases or official sites.
3. **Trace numbers** to the original publication (see [source evaluation](source-evaluation.md)).
4. **Cross-check** important claims with an independent search.
5. **Mark** in your notes what was verified and what was not.

### Prompting for research

- State the context and the level of detail you need.
- Ask the model to distinguish what it is confident about from what it is unsure about, and to say when it does not know (it may still be wrong).
- Ask for counterarguments and limitations.
- Provide the documents you want summarized, rather than relying on its memory.
- Ask for search queries you can run yourself.

### Privacy, confidentiality and rules

- Check whether the tool stores and uses inputs for training, and use enterprise or privacy settings where needed.
- Do not enter personal data, client secrets or confidential documents without a legal basis and approval.
- Follow your employer's and your publisher's rules on AI use, and disclose substantial AI assistance where expected.
- In this library, AI-generated practice questions are marked with `origin: "ai"` and `reviewed: false` until accepted (see [GUIDELINE](../../../../GUIDELINE.md#35-sidecar-metadata)).

## Worked example

A marketing manager researches "the effect of free shipping thresholds on average order value".

1. **Orientation:** the AI assistant explains the concept and lists factors (threshold level, product mix, customer type), and suggests search terms including "free shipping threshold", "contingent free shipping" and "minimum order for free shipping".
2. **Sources suggested:** the assistant names three academic papers. Searching the titles in Google Scholar: one exists with the stated authors, one exists with a different year and journal, one cannot be found. Only the first two are kept, with corrected details.
3. **Original search:** a Google Scholar search for "contingent free shipping" finds research the assistant did not mention.
4. **Reading:** the manager reads the abstracts and key results of the verified papers and notes their contexts (countries, years, product categories).
5. **Synthesis:** the manager writes the summary in their own words with verified citations, using the assistant to improve the structure of the text.

The example describes a typical pattern, not a specific test of a specific tool.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- **Speed versus reliability.** AI speeds up early steps and drafting. Verification takes time and cannot be skipped for important claims.
- **Convenience versus understanding.** Relying on summaries can leave gaps in your own understanding. Read key sources yourself.
- **Web-connected versus offline models.** Web-connected tools are more current and cite sources, which still need checking.
- **Confidentiality.** The more sensitive the material, the more the tool's data terms matter.

## Common mistakes

- **Citing AI-provided references without opening them.** Fix: verify each one.
- **Treating fluency as accuracy.** Fix: check facts in primary sources.
- **Using AI for current rules and prices without checking.** Fix: go to official sources.
- **Uploading confidential data carelessly.** Fix: check terms and policies.
- **Not recording what was verified.** Fix: mark verified and unverified claims.
