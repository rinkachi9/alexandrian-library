# Specialized sources

## Summary

General search engines are the right start for many questions and the wrong place for others. Research findings live in scholarly databases, statistics in official statistics offices, laws in legal databases, company data in public registers, and historical material in library and newspaper archives. These specialized sources are more complete and reliable within their domain, often free, and searchable with more precise tools than a web search. Knowing where each kind of information is kept is a core research skill, together with knowing how to reach paywalled research legally and how to check whether a study was retracted.

## Prerequisites

[Search strategy](search-strategy.md) and [source evaluation](source-evaluation.md).

## Core concepts

### Definition

A *specialized source* is a database, archive or repository that collects a particular kind of information with defined coverage and quality control, such as peer-reviewed literature, official statistics, legislation or company filings.

*Analogy.* A general search engine is like a city's main square: almost everything passes through it at some point, but it is noisy and nobody checks what people shout. Specialized sources are the city's archives, courts, statistics office and university libraries: quieter, organized, with rules about what gets in. For a serious question you start at the square to get your bearings and then go to the right building. The analogy breaks because many of these "buildings" now have doors on the square (results from them appear in web search), but only partially, so you still need to go inside for complete results.

### Research literature

| Source | What it covers | Access |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Google Scholar | Broad coverage of scholarly literature, citations | Free |
| PubMed | Biomedical and life sciences | Free, abstracts and links |
| Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex | Scholarly literature with metadata | Free |
| Web of Science, Scopus | Curated citation databases | Subscription, often via universities |
| DOAJ | Directory of open access journals | Free |
| Cochrane Library | Systematic reviews in health care | Partly free |
| arXiv, SSRN, OSF Preprints | Preprints (not yet peer reviewed) | Free |

**Getting full text legally:** many articles have free versions. Tools such as Unpaywall find legal open access copies. Authors' websites, institutional repositories and PubMed Central host many papers. Libraries provide access and interlibrary loan. Authors usually send a copy on request.

**Retractions:** check whether a paper has been retracted or corrected. The Retraction Watch database, acquired by Crossref in 2023 and made openly available, lists retractions. Journal pages and databases usually mark retracted papers.

**Preprints** are useful for new findings, and have not passed peer review. Treat them with more caution.

### Official statistics

- **International:** Eurostat (EU), OECD, World Bank, United Nations agencies, International Labour Organization.
- **National:** statistics offices such as GUS (Statistics Poland), Destatis (Germany), the US Census Bureau and the Bureau of Labor Statistics.
- **Central banks** for financial and economic data.

Read the methodology: definitions (for example of unemployment or retail e-commerce), revisions and seasonal adjustment change the meaning of numbers.

### Law and official documents

- **EU law:** EUR-Lex (legislation, case law references), CURIA (Court of Justice judgments).
- **National law:** in Poland ISAP (Internetowy System Aktów Prawnych) and the official journal Dziennik Ustaw; in Germany gesetze-im-internet.de; in the US the eCFR and govinfo.gov.
- **Regulators' guidance:** data protection authorities, consumer protection authorities (UOKiK in Poland, the FTC in the US).

Always check the consolidated (current) version of a law and the date it applies from.

### Companies and organizations

- **Company registers:** in Poland KRS (National Court Register) and CEIDG for sole traders; national registers elsewhere; the EU's Business Registers Interconnection System.
- **Financial filings:** annual reports, stock exchange filings (for example SEC EDGAR in the US).
- **Trademarks and patents:** EUIPO, national patent offices, WIPO databases, Google Patents.

### Archives and historical sources

- **Web archives:** the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine for past versions of web pages.
- **Newspapers:** national libraries' digital collections (Polona of the National Library of Poland, Europeana, the Library of Congress's Chronicling America for US newspapers). Google's News Archive is fixed since 2011 (see [search operators](search-operators.md)).
- **Books:** Google Books, HathiTrust, national library catalogs.

### Standards and technical documentation

- Standards bodies (ISO, CEN, national bodies) publish catalogs. Standards themselves are often paid.
- Official product documentation (software, platforms) is the primary source for how a tool works and changes faster than books.
- RFCs (rfc-editor.org) for internet protocols.

### Choosing the source

| Question | First source |
| --- | --- |
| What does research say about X? | Systematic reviews (Cochrane, PubMed, Google Scholar) |
| How many people or businesses...? | Official statistics office |
| What does the law require? | Official legal database, regulator guidance |
| Who owns or runs this company? | Company register |
| What did this page say in 2019? | Wayback Machine |
| What did newspapers report in 1920? | National library digital collections |

## Worked example

Question: "What share of retail sales in the EU is online, and how has it changed?"

1. **General search** returns many marketing blogs with conflicting numbers and no methods.
2. **Eurostat:** search Eurostat for "e-commerce sales" finds data on the share of enterprises' turnover from e-commerce and on individuals buying online, with definitions.
3. **Definitions matter:** Eurostat measures e-commerce sales of enterprises (including B2B) and the share of individuals who bought online, not a single "online share of retail". A national statistics office may publish retail-specific figures.
4. **Cross-check:** compare with the US Census Bureau's quarterly retail e-commerce series for context, noting different definitions.
5. **Conclusion:** report the Eurostat indicator with its exact definition and year, and note that it is not directly comparable with the US figure.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- **Specialized sources take learning.** Each database has its own interface and terms. The investment pays off for repeated research.
- **Paywalls.** Use legal open access routes and libraries.
- **Currency.** Official statistics and peer-reviewed research lag behind events. News and preprints are faster and less reliable.

## Common mistakes

- **Using blogs for statistics.** Fix: go to the statistics office.
- **Citing a law without checking the current version.** Fix: use consolidated texts and application dates.
- **Ignoring retractions.** Fix: check the paper's status.
- **Treating preprints as established.** Fix: label them as not peer reviewed.
- **Comparing numbers with different definitions.** Fix: read the methodology.

## Practice

1. Name the best first source for: the unemployment rate in your country, the text of a regulation, a company's board members, and the evidence on a medical treatment.
2. How can you find a legal free version of a paywalled article?
3. Check whether a well-known study in your field has been corrected or retracted.
4. Find a past version of a company's homepage in the Wayback Machine and describe what changed.
