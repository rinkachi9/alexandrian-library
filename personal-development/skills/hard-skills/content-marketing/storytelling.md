# Storytelling

## Summary

Storytelling in marketing is the use of narrative, a character who wants something, meets an obstacle and changes, to make a message memorable and persuasive. Research on narrative transportation shows that people absorbed in a story are more likely to adopt the beliefs it implies and less likely to argue against them, and a meta-analysis of 76 articles confirmed this effect for consumers. Stories work best when the customer, not the company, is the hero, when details are concrete and true, and when the story serves a clear point. A story that is invented, manipulative or unrelated to the product damages trust.

## Core concepts

### Definition

A *story* is an account of a character who wants something, faces an obstacle and, through action, reaches a changed state. *Storytelling* in content marketing is the use of such accounts (customer stories, founder stories, product origin stories, case studies) to convey a message. What separates a story from a list of facts is causality and change over time: this happened, therefore that, and someone was different at the end.

*Analogy.* Facts are like bricks lying on the ground: each is solid, but you cannot walk into a pile of bricks. A story is the same bricks built into a house with a door: the reader enters, moves from room to room and remembers the layout. The analogy breaks because a house stays standing whatever the visitor believes, while a story only works if the listener accepts it as plausible, so a single false detail can bring the whole thing down.

### Why stories persuade: narrative transportation

Melanie Green and Timothy Brock (2000) described *narrative transportation* as a state in which a reader becomes absorbed in a story: attention, imagery and feelings are focused on the narrative world. In four experiments they found that more transported readers held beliefs more consistent with the story and found fewer false notes in it, whether the story was labeled fact or fiction.

Van Laer, de Ruyter, Visconti and Wetzels (2014) meta-analyzed 132 effect sizes from 76 articles. Transportation was associated with stronger story-consistent beliefs, attitudes and intentions, more affective responses and fewer critical thoughts. Factors linked to higher transportation included an identifiable character, an imaginable plot and verisimilitude (the story feels true to life), as well as the reader's familiarity with the genre and attention.

The mechanism has an implication that cuts both ways: stories reduce counterarguing. That is why they persuade, and why they can be used to manipulate.

### Structure of a story

Many story models describe the same core. A simple one for marketing:

1. **Character.** A person the audience can identify with, usually the customer.
2. **Desire.** What they want.
3. **Obstacle.** What stands in the way (the problem).
4. **Guide.** Someone or something that helps (the company, the product, a method).
5. **Action and turning point.** What they did and what changed.
6. **Resolution.** The new situation, with concrete detail.
7. **Meaning.** What the audience should take away.

Donald Miller's practitioner framework *StoryBrand* (2017) popularized the advice that the customer should be the hero and the brand the guide. The underlying point is consistent with the transportation research: readers are transported when they can identify with the character.

### Types of marketing stories

| Type | Purpose | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer story or case study | Proof that the product works for someone like the reader | A bakery that cut waste by a third with an ordering app |
| Founder or origin story | Explain why the company exists and what it values | Engineers tired of warm office water |
| Product or process story | Make a feature tangible | How a tea is picked, rolled and dried |
| Mission or cause story | Connect with shared values | A shop that funds tree planting |
| Teaching story | Make a lesson memorable | A mistake that cost a customer money, and how to avoid it |

### Craft principles

- **Concrete details.** Names, places, numbers and sensory details make a story imaginable. "Maria runs a 12 seat bakery in Kraków" beats "a small business owner".
- **Real conflict.** A story without an obstacle is an announcement.
- **Show change.** Before and after, ideally with numbers.
- **One point per story.** Decide what the audience should believe or do afterwards.
- **Truth.** Use real stories with permission, or label hypothetical ones as illustrations. In the EU, presenting an invented customer as real can be a misleading commercial practice.

### Story formats by channel

- **Articles and case studies:** full arc with detail and data.
- **Video:** character and emotion in the first seconds, resolution shown, not told.
- **Social posts:** compressed arc (before, after, bridge, see [copywriting frameworks](../copywriting/copywriting-frameworks.md#bab)).
- **Sales conversations:** short customer stories that answer objections.

## Worked example

A bookkeeping app writes a customer story.

**Facts collected in an interview (with permission):** Anna, a freelance illustrator in Gdańsk, used to spend the last weekend of each quarter sorting receipts. In 2025 she missed a deadline and paid a penalty of 300 PLN. She started using the app, photographs receipts when she gets them, and now closes her quarter in about 30 minutes.

**Weak version:** "Our app helps freelancers save time on bookkeeping. Anna is very happy with it."

**Story version:**

> Every quarter Anna, an illustrator in Gdańsk, lost a weekend to a shoebox of receipts. In April 2025 she lost something else: a 300 PLN penalty for a late filing, because a client's invoice was buried under coffee receipts.
>
> "I am good with deadlines for clients. I was terrible with my own," she says.
>
> Now she photographs each receipt in the app the moment she gets it. At the end of June she closed the quarter in 30 minutes, on a Tuesday evening, and spent the weekend drawing.

Check: character (Anna), desire (time for her work), obstacle (receipts, penalty), guide (the app, mentioned in one line), change (30 minutes, a free weekend), concrete details (city, amount, date). The story is invented for this example. A real one needs the customer's consent to publication.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- **Memorable but slow.** Stories take more words than claims. Use them where trust and memory matter, keep short claims for scanning readers.
- **Less counterarguing cuts both ways.** It helps a true message and makes a misleading one more dangerous. Use the power honestly.
- **One story is anecdotal.** A case study is proof for "this is possible", not for "this is typical". Pair it with data where you can.
- **Not every piece needs a story.** Reference material (shipping times, specifications) should be plain and fast.

## Common mistakes

- **The company as hero.** Fix: make the customer the character who changes.
- **No obstacle.** Fix: show what was hard before.
- **Vague details.** Fix: names, numbers, places (with permission).
- **Invented testimonials presented as real.** Fix: use real stories, or clearly label illustrations.
- **A story without a point.** Fix: decide the takeaway before writing.
