# Capturing attention

## Summary

Beyond the headline, attention depends on what the reader sees and feels in the first seconds: images, page design, speed and signs that other people trust the content. People judge a site's credibility largely by its look, slow pages lose visitors before they see anything, and good content still needs promotion because nobody can share what they have not seen. Many popular claims in this area, such as "the brain processes images 60,000 times faster than text", have no traceable source, so this note separates the evidence from the folklore.

## Core concepts

### What attention means here

*Attention*, in content marketing, is the reader's decision to keep looking at a piece instead of moving on. It is limited: a reader on a phone feed or a search results page compares your content with many alternatives in a few seconds. Capturing attention means passing that first quick judgment. Holding it means the content then delivers.

*Analogy.* Think of a market street. The headline is the shop sign, the images and design are the window display and the tidiness of the shop, speed is whether the door opens when you push it, and social proof is the queue outside. A shopper walks in only if all of these look right. The analogy breaks because online the shopper can leave in one click and never comes back to tell you why.

### Images

People extract meaning from images very quickly. In a study by Potter, Wyble, Hagmann and McCourt (2014), participants could detect whether a picture matched a description (such as "picnic") even when pictures were shown for only 13 milliseconds each in a rapid sequence. This supports the general idea that images are processed fast. It does not support the often repeated claim that the brain processes images "60,000 times faster than text". That figure has no known research source and should not be used.

Practical guidance from the source notes:

- Use images that support the message and fit its tone.
- Avoid graphic or repulsive images. They draw the eye but push readers away.
- Shareable visuals (charts, memes, quote cards) travel well on platforms built around images.
- Keep images consistent with the brand and with each other.

### Site design and credibility

In a Stanford study of how people judge the credibility of websites (Fogg and colleagues, 2002 and 2003), 2,684 participants compared live sites and wrote comments. The most frequently mentioned factor was the "design look" of the site, in 46.1% of the comments, followed by information design and structure (about 28.5%). People said they cared about expertise and accuracy, but what they actually commented on first was appearance. A cluttered or dated page signals that the content is not maintained, even when it is good.

Key elements:

- **Visual quality.** A clean, current design that looks maintained.
- **Usability.** Clear structure, readable type, obvious navigation.
- **Mobile layout.** Most visits to consumer content come from phones, so a page must work on a small screen.

### Speed

A slow page loses readers before they see the content. Google reported in 2017 that the probability of a visitor leaving (a *bounce*) increases by 32% as page load time goes from 1 second to 3 seconds, based on its analysis of mobile sites. Google also uses page experience signals, measured by the *Core Web Vitals*, in search (see [SEO fundamentals](../search-engine-optimization/seo-fundamentals.md#technical-seo)).

### Promotion and social proof

Good content does not spread by itself. The source notes list three levers:

- **Collaboration with influencers**, people who already have the attention of your audience.
- **Making sharing easy**, and asking for it.
- **Social proof**, visible signs that others trust you: testimonials, reviews, numbers of readers or customers.

Social proof is one of the persuasion principles described in [persuasion principles](../copywriting/persuasion-principles.md#social-proof), where its evidence and limits are discussed.

### Psychological principles from the source notes

The source lists four principles. Their status differs:

| Principle | Idea | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Curiosity gap | Give enough to intrigue, hold back enough to make people want more | Grounded in information gap theory (Loewenstein, 1994). See [headlines](headlines.md#mechanism-benefit-and-curiosity). |
| Fear of missing out (FOMO) | Emphasize exclusivity or limited availability | Related to scarcity research. Misleading scarcity is illegal in the EU and the US. |
| Priming | The headline and image frame how the content is read | Framing effects are established. Many social priming findings failed to replicate, so do not expect large effects from subtle cues. |
| Cognitive fluency | Simpler design and language are easier to process and feel more trustworthy | Supported by research on processing fluency (for example Alter and Oppenheimer, 2009). |

### Ethics: promise and deliver

Every technique above can be used to pull attention to content that does not deserve it. That works once. Consistently delivering what you promise is what keeps an audience.

### Practical checklist

- Specific headline with a clear benefit.
- Curiosity paired with usefulness.
- Urgency only when it is real.
- An image that supports the message.
- A clean, fast, mobile-friendly page.
- Active promotion and visible, honest social proof.

## Worked example

A blog post on budgeting for students gets traffic from social media, but 85% of visitors leave within ten seconds. An audit finds:

1. The page takes about 5 seconds to load on a mid-range phone because of a 4 MB header photo. Fix: compress the image to under 200 KB and lazy-load images below the fold.
2. The header photo is a generic stock image of coins. Fix: replace it with a screenshot of the actual budget template the post offers.
3. The first screen shows a pop-up asking for an email address before any content. Fix: move the sign-up offer to the end of the post.
4. There is no sign that anyone has used the template. Fix: add one short, real testimonial from a student who used it, with permission.

After the changes, the team compares the share of visitors who scroll past the first screen for two weeks before and after. The numbers in this example are invented. A before and after comparison is weaker evidence than an A/B test, because other things change at the same time, such as the season or the traffic source.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- **Design costs money and time,** but the credibility research suggests that a dated look costs trust.
- **Rich media versus speed.** Large images and video attract attention but slow the page. Optimize them or load them after the main content.
- **Promotion versus production.** A common split in practice is to spend as much effort promoting a piece as producing it. This is a rule of thumb without a research source.
- **Social proof can backfire** when the numbers are small ("3 people bought this") or when it signals that an undesirable behavior is common.

## Common mistakes

- **Citing made-up statistics,** such as the "60,000 times faster" claim. Fix: trace every number to its source or drop it.
- **Interrupting readers before they get value,** for example with immediate pop-ups. Fix: let the content earn the request.
- **Fake urgency.** Fix: use deadlines and limits only when they are real.
- **Publishing and waiting.** Fix: plan promotion before publishing.
