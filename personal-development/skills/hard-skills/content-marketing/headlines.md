# Headlines

## Summary

A headline has one job: to make the right reader stop and start reading. It does that by promising a clear benefit, by opening a gap in what the reader knows, or both, and it only works long term if the content keeps the promise. Headlines are cheap to test, and the largest public dataset of headline tests shows that small wording changes often move click rates, but in ways that are hard to predict. Write the headline first to focus the piece, then test and revise it.

## Prerequisites

[Content marketing](content-marketing-fundamentals.md), and the attention step of the [7A framework](seven-a-framework.md#attention).

## Core concepts

### The role of a headline

*Definition.* A *headline* is the title text that a reader sees before deciding whether to open a piece of content: an article title, an email subject line, a video title or the first line of a social post. The *lead* (the first lines or seconds) continues the work of the headline and carries the reader into the body.

*Analogy.* A headline is a shop window. People walking past decide in a second whether to enter. A window full of everything is ignored, and a window that promises something the shop does not sell gets people in once and never again. The analogy breaks because a shop window is seen by everyone walking by, while a headline is often shown by an algorithm to a selected audience, so the same headline can perform differently in different channels.

### Mechanism: benefit and curiosity

Two forces make people open content:

- **Expected benefit.** The reader expects to gain something: save money, solve a problem, avoid a mistake. "How to save 500 dollars a month with five habits" states the benefit directly.
- **Curiosity.** George Loewenstein's information gap theory (1994) describes curiosity as a feeling of deprivation that arises when attention is drawn to a gap between what one knows and what one wants to know. Curiosity needs some prior knowledge: people are not curious about a topic they know nothing about. A headline such as "Are you making these common grammar mistakes?" opens a gap for anyone who writes.

The source notes call the combination *meaning plus fascination*: the content solves a problem or meets a desire (meaning), and the headline adds intrigue (fascination). What counts as meaningful is decided by the audience, not by the marketer, which is why audience research comes before headline writing.

### Techniques from the source notes

- **Numbers.** "7 ways to..." signals a concrete, scannable structure. The source suggests that precise numbers (such as 70.42%) add credibility. This is practitioner advice without a research source here.
- **Clear benefit.** Say what the reader gets.
- **Clarity over cleverness.** Puns and wordplay can hide the value. The reader should understand the offer at a glance.
- **Curiosity with a purpose.** Pair the gap with a benefit, so the reader knows why closing the gap is worth it.
- **Urgency and scarcity.** "Last chance to register" can prompt action, but only when it is true. See [persuasion principles](../copywriting/persuasion-principles.md#scarcity) for the evidence and the legal limits.

### Best practices for writing

- **Write the headline first.** It forces you to decide what the piece promises and keeps the writing focused. Revise it when the piece is finished.
- **Study and adapt good headlines.** Look at publications with large audiences and adapt the structure, not the words.
- **Look outside your niche.** Inspiration from another field is less likely to sound like your competitors. If you write about fitness, read headlines from finance or travel.
- **Use formulas sparingly.** Overused, hype-heavy formulas reduce credibility.
- **Match the audience.** A new blog may need bold promises to be noticed, while experienced readers trust restrained headlines more. The same word ("breakthrough") can help with one audience and hurt with another.
- **Test.** Compare email open rates, click rates or engagement for different versions (see [A/B testing](../copywriting/ab-testing.md)).

### What headline tests show

The Upworthy Research Archive (Matias, Munger, Aubin Le Quere and Ebersole, 2021) contains 32,487 headline and image tests that the media site Upworthy ran between January 2013 and April 2015, with more than 538 million randomly assigned viewers. It is the largest public record of such tests. Two lessons follow from the existence and use of this archive:

1. Editors at Upworthy tested many headline variants per story because they could not reliably predict the winner. Intuition is a weak guide.
2. Results from one audience do not transfer automatically. Upworthy's readers in 2013 to 2015 are not your readers.

In 2024 the authors reported possible randomization problems in about 22% of the tests and published guidance on which tests to use. This is a reminder that test data needs checking too.

### Promise and deliver

*Clickbait* is a headline that promises more than the content delivers. It can raise clicks once, but it teaches readers that your headlines are not trustworthy. The source notes put it this way: make sure your headline is not writing a check that your content cannot cash.

## Worked example

Content: an article explaining how to read a nutrition label in under a minute.

| Version | Problem | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "Labels" | No benefit, no reader, no curiosity | Weak |
| "A label-reading revolution you won't believe" | Hype, vague, promises more than the article gives | Clickbait |
| "How to read a nutrition label in 60 seconds" | Clear benefit and a concrete number | Good |
| "The 3 numbers on a nutrition label that matter most" | Benefit plus curiosity (which three?) | Good, test against the previous one |

The last two are both honest. An A/B test on the email subject line would decide between them for this audience.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- **Curiosity versus clarity.** Curiosity gaps can raise clicks but annoy readers if overused. Clear benefit headlines are safer for search, where the reader typed a question and wants a direct answer.
- **Channel matters.** Search results reward headlines that match the query (see [keyword research](../search-engine-optimization/keyword-research.md)). Social feeds reward headlines that stand out. Email subject lines compete with the inbox.
- **Testing needs traffic.** With a small audience, differences between headlines are buried in noise. Use judgment and principles until you have enough traffic.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing the headline last, in a hurry.** Fix: draft it first and revise it at the end.
- **Promising what the content does not deliver.** Fix: read the headline again after finishing the piece and adjust it to the content.
- **Copying a formula word for word.** Fix: adapt the structure and use the language of your own audience.
- **Declaring a winner after a few dozen clicks.** Fix: decide the sample size before testing.
