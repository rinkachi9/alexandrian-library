# Positioning

## Summary

Positioning is deciding what you want to be known for, by whom, and how you differ from others who offer something similar. In marketing, Al Ries and Jack Trout described positioning as owning a clear place in the mind of the audience. For a person, a good position combines a real strength, a specific audience with a real need, and a difference that matters to that audience. It is expressed in a short positioning statement that guides your profile, content and choices of work. A narrow position is easier to remember and recommend, and can be widened later from a base of reputation.

## Core concepts

### Definition

*Positioning* is the act of designing an offer and image so that it occupies a distinct and valued place in the minds of a target audience. Applied to a person, it answers three questions:

1. **What problem do I solve or what value do I bring?**
2. **For whom?**
3. **Why me rather than others?**

*Analogy.* Positioning is like choosing a seat at a long conference table where everyone offers similar services. Sitting in the middle among twenty people with the same badge, nobody remembers you. Sitting at the end labeled "for logistics companies with delivery cost problems", the right people walk straight to you, and others know where to send them. The analogy breaks because the table is in people's minds: you can choose where you want to sit, but you only own the seat once people consistently put you there, which takes time and evidence.

### Origin

Al Ries and Jack Trout introduced positioning in advertising articles in 1972 and in the book *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* (1981). Their argument: people are flooded with messages and remember only a few clear, simple positions in each category, so a brand should own one distinct idea rather than claim to be good at everything. Being first in a category, or defining a new, narrower category, makes this easier.

### The three circles

A practical way to find a position combines:

| Circle | Question | Sources |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strengths | What am I good at, with evidence? | Results, feedback, [skills inventory](../../../career/self-assessment/skills-inventory-and-gap-analysis.md) |
| Audience need | Who needs this and will value it? | Market research, conversations, job postings, client problems |
| Difference | What do I offer that comparable people do not? | Unusual combination of skills, industry experience, method, perspective |

The position lies where all three overlap.

### Ways to differentiate

- **Audience:** serving a specific industry or type of client ("for dental practices").
- **Problem:** focusing on one problem ("checkouts that lose mobile buyers").
- **Skill combination:** two or three skills rarely found together ("lawyer who can code").
- **Method or approach:** a distinctive way of working ("data-first", "workshops instead of reports").
- **Perspective or values:** a recognizable point of view ("sustainable e-commerce").

### Positioning statement

A short internal statement guides decisions:

> For [audience] who [need or problem], I am the [category] who [main benefit], because [proof or difference].

Example: "For mid-sized logistics companies struggling with rising delivery costs, I am the data analyst who finds savings in delivery operations within three months, because I have done it for two carriers and combine operations experience with analytics."

The statement is not a slogan. Public versions (a profile headline, a website tagline) are shorter.

### Niche fears

Common worry: "If I narrow down, I lose opportunities." In practice, a clear position makes it easier for people to remember and recommend you, and many people with a niche still take work outside it. A position is also not permanent: people often start narrow, build a reputation, and widen. These observations come from practitioner experience, not controlled studies.

### Testing a position

- Describe it to ten people from the target audience. Do they understand it immediately? Do they know someone who needs it?
- Search for others with the same position. If many exist, sharpen the difference.
- Check demand: are there job postings, client requests or discussions about the problem?
- Watch what happens after you start using it: who contacts you, and how do they describe you?

## Worked example

A graphic designer with eight years of general experience repositions (invented details).

1. **Strengths:** packaging projects get the best feedback. Strong knowledge of print production. Several clients in food.
2. **Audience need:** small food producers launching products in supermarkets need packaging that meets labeling rules and stands out on shelves. Conversations with five producers confirm the problem.
3. **Difference:** combines design with knowledge of EU food labeling requirements and print production, so packaging goes from idea to print without legal or technical problems.
4. **Positioning statement:** "For small food producers preparing for supermarket listing, I design packaging that sells on the shelf and passes labeling checks, because I combine packaging design with food labeling and print production experience."
5. **Public headline:** "Packaging design for small food brands | Shelf-ready and label-compliant".
6. **Consequences:** portfolio reorganized around food packaging, an article on common labeling mistakes, outreach to food producer associations.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- **Specific versus flexible.** Specific positions are memorable. Flexible ones keep options. Most people benefit from being specific in public and flexible in what they accept.
- **Category creation versus existing category.** A new category ("shelf-ready packaging") can be distinctive and needs explanation.
- **Positioning on price** ("cheapest") is fragile: someone can always be cheaper.
- **Changing position** costs time, because reputations move slowly.

## Common mistakes

- **Positioning on generic qualities** ("hard-working", "creative"). Fix: name the audience and the problem.
- **Choosing a niche without demand.** Fix: test with the audience.
- **Claiming a difference without proof.** Fix: show evidence.
- **Changing position every few months.** Fix: give it a year and adjust gradually.
