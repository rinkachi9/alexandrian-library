# Copywriting

## Summary

Copywriting is writing whose purpose is to make a specific reader take a specific action, such as buying, signing up or calling. Good copy comes mostly from research: knowing the reader's problem and words so well that the text describes their situation better than they could. It then turns product features into benefits the reader cares about, removes doubts, and ends with one clear next step. Web readers scan more than they read, so copy must also be concise and easy to scan.

## Prerequisites

None. The audience ideas in [content marketing](../content-marketing/content-marketing.md) help.

## Core concepts

### Definition

*Copywriting* is the writing of persuasive text for marketing and sales: product pages, advertisements, emails, landing pages, scripts. *Direct response copywriting* is the branch that asks for an immediate, measurable action ("Order now", "Start your free trial") and is judged by the result of that action. Content writing informs or entertains first. Copywriting persuades first. In practice the two overlap, because the end of a content piece usually contains copy.

*Analogy.* A copywriter is like a good salesperson in a shop who listens first and talks second. They find out what the customer needs, show the one product that fits, answer the worry the customer has not voiced yet and then say where the till is. The analogy breaks because the written salesperson cannot hear the customer's answer, so all the listening must happen before writing, through research.

### Research first

The only note in the source export under copywriting is a single value: *obsessed*. Read as a working principle, it means being obsessed with the customer and the product: reading reviews, support tickets, forum posts and interview notes until you know the reader's problem, their objections and their exact phrases. Many experienced copywriters describe research as most of the work. Copy that uses the reader's own words feels written for them.

Useful research sources:

- product reviews (your own and competitors'), especially three-star reviews, which often explain both what people wanted and what disappointed them,
- support emails and sales call notes,
- customer interviews (see [product validation](../e-commerce/product-validation.md#customer-interviews)),
- search queries that bring people to the page.

### Features and benefits

A *feature* is a fact about the product: "450 W motor", "14 speed settings", "1.4 l glass jar". A *benefit* is what the feature does for the reader: "crushes ice for smoothies in 30 seconds". Readers buy benefits, but they need features as evidence that the benefit is real. A simple method is to write each feature and then ask "so what?" until you reach something the reader cares about.

| Feature | So what? | Benefit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 450 W motor | It is strong enough for ice and frozen fruit | Smoothies without lumps |
| Glass jar | Glass does not absorb smells or scratch like many plastics | The jar still looks and smells new after a year |
| Dishwasher-safe parts | No hand washing | Cleaning takes one minute |

The source notes describe benefits as *emotional* (how the reader will feel: less stress, more confidence) and *logical* (facts, figures and evidence that justify the decision). The common claim that "people decide emotionally and justify logically" is a simplification of research on the role of emotion in decisions. It is a useful writing reminder, not an established law. Use both kinds of benefit.

### Structures

Copywriters use a few standard structures to order a persuasive text.

- **AIDA:** Attention, Interest, Desire, Action. The reader is moved from noticing to wanting to acting. Edward K. Strong attributed the formula to the advertising practitioner E. St. Elmo Lewis in 1925 (*The Psychology of Selling and Advertising*), dating it to 1898. Historians have since questioned that attribution.
- **PAS:** Problem, Agitate, Solution. Name the problem, make its cost felt, then offer the product as the solution. It is a practitioner formula with no research source.
- **The five step formula of the source notes:** establish trust and authority, describe the product, emphasize emotional and logical benefits, paint a picture of the transformation, end with a call to action. Steps 4 and 5 and the handling of risk are described in [calls to action and risk reversal](calls-to-action-and-risk-reversal.md).

These are checklists for order, not guarantees. Their value is that they prevent common gaps, such as a page with no call to action, or a call to action before the reader knows what the product does.

### Writing for screens

Readers on the web scan. In usability tests reported by Jakob Nielsen in 1997, 79% of test users always scanned a new page and only 16% read word by word. In the companion study by Morkes and Nielsen, a sample website became measurably more usable when its text was rewritten to be concise (58% better), scannable (47%) and objective (27%), and 124% better with all three. That study used one sample site about tourism in Nebraska, so treat the percentages as an illustration, not as a general law.

Practical rules that follow:

- Lead with the conclusion or the main benefit.
- Use descriptive subheadings, short paragraphs and lists for parallel items.
- Cut adjectives that make claims without evidence ("world class", "revolutionary").
- Use specific numbers instead of vague claims.
- One idea per paragraph, one call to action per page.

### Voice

The voice is how the copy sounds: formal or casual, playful or serious. It should match the brand and the reader, and stay the same across pages. A consistent voice is part of being trustworthy (see *authentic* in the [7A framework](../content-marketing/seven-a-framework.md#authentic)).

### Ethics and law

Persuasion must stay truthful. In the EU, the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (2005/29/EC) prohibits misleading and aggressive commercial practices, and in the US the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) Act prohibits deceptive advertising. Claims about health, money or results need evidence. Testimonials must be real and represent typical experience or say clearly that they do not. This note is educational and is not legal advice.

## Worked example

Brief: a product page for a refillable stainless steel water bottle, aimed at office workers.

**Weak copy:** "Our revolutionary premium bottle is the best on the market. Order now."

Problems: no reader, no benefit, an unsupported superlative, and a call to action before the reader knows anything.

**Revised copy, structured with the five step formula:**

1. *Trust:* "Designed by two engineers who were tired of lukewarm office water. Used by 12,000 customers since 2023."
2. *Product:* "A 750 ml double-walled steel bottle with a leak-proof lid that fits standard cup holders."
3. *Benefits:* "Cold water for 24 hours, so you stop buying bottled water at lunch (logical: about 2 euros a day saved). No more condensation rings on your desk or wet laptop bag (emotional: one less irritation)."
4. *Transformation:* "Fill it at home, and it is still cold at your 4 pm meeting."
5. *Call to action and risk reversal:* "Order today. If it leaks in the first year, we replace it."

All numbers and claims in the example are invented. On a real page each would need evidence.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- **Long or short copy.** Expensive or unfamiliar products need more explanation and evidence. Cheap, familiar products need less. Test rather than assume.
- **Formulas versus voice.** Structures prevent gaps but can make copy sound generic. Use them to check a draft, not to write it.
- **Persuasion versus trust.** Pressure tactics can raise short-term conversions and cost returning customers. See [persuasion principles](persuasion-principles.md).
- **Copy cannot fix a bad product** or a bad offer. If research shows the product does not solve the reader's problem, change the product or the audience.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing before researching.** Fix: collect the reader's words first.
- **Listing features only.** Fix: apply "so what?" to each feature.
- **Unsupported superlatives.** Fix: replace each with a fact or delete it.
- **Several calls to action competing on one page.** Fix: choose one primary action.
- **Walls of text.** Fix: subheadings, short paragraphs, lists.
