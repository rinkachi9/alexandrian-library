# Customer research for copywriting

## Summary

Customer research for copywriting is the systematic collection of what buyers want, fear, compare and say, in their own words, before any copy is written. Its main sources are reviews, support conversations, interviews, surveys, search queries and sales calls. Two ideas organize the findings: the job the customer is trying to get done, and how aware the reader already is of their problem and of possible solutions. Copy built from this research uses the reader's language, addresses their real objections and meets them at their level of awareness, which is the main difference between copy that converts and copy that only sounds good.

## Core concepts

### Definition

*Customer research* (for copy) is the collection and analysis of evidence about a target reader's situation, goals, problems, alternatives, objections, decision criteria and vocabulary. The output is not a report but a set of decisions and phrases for copy: who the reader is, what they want, what stops them and what words they use. The common practitioner name for the collected phrases is *voice of customer* (VoC).

*Analogy.* Research is to copy what a survey of the ground is to building. You can design a beautiful house without surveying, but it may stand on sand or face the wrong way. The survey does not design the house, but every good design decision depends on it. The analogy breaks because ground does not change between survey and building, while customers' language and priorities shift with markets and seasons, so research needs refreshing.

### The problem it solves

Writers naturally describe products the way the company sees them: features, internal names, technical advantages. Buyers think in problems, situations and outcomes, and use different words. A copywriter who writes from imagination produces copy that is accurate but does not connect. Research replaces imagination with evidence.

### Sources of customer insight

| Source | What it reveals | Strength | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reviews (yours and competitors') | Outcomes people value, disappointments, exact phrases | Real buyers, large volume, free | Skewed toward very happy and very unhappy buyers |
| Support tickets and chat logs | Confusions, objections, failures | Real problems in real time | Skewed toward problems |
| Sales call notes and recordings | Objections, decision criteria, competitors considered | Shows the decision process | Filtered by the salesperson |
| Customer interviews | Context, motivations, the story of the purchase | Depth, follow-up questions | Small samples, memory bias |
| Surveys | Patterns across many customers | Scale | Shallow, stated preferences (see [product validation](../e-commerce/product-validation.md#why-stated-intent-is-weak-evidence)) |
| Search queries and autocomplete | How people phrase problems before they know solutions | Unprompted language | No context about the person |
| Forums and communities | Unfiltered discussion of problems and alternatives | Honest, detailed | Not representative |

### Review mining

*Review mining* is a structured reading of reviews to extract recurring themes and phrases.

1. Collect 50 to 200 reviews of your product and of two or three competitors.
2. Read three-star and four-star reviews first. They often explain both what the buyer wanted and what disappointed them.
3. Copy exact phrases into a spreadsheet with columns: theme (outcome, problem, objection, comparison), phrase, source, rating.
4. Count themes. The most frequent outcomes become candidate promises. The most frequent disappointments with competitors become differentiators. The most frequent doubts become objections to answer.

### Customer interviews

Interviews with recent buyers (and with people who considered the product and did not buy) reveal the story of the decision. Good practice, consistent with Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* (2013):

- Ask about specific past events: "Tell me about the day you decided to look for a new bottle."
- Ask what they tried before, what it cost them and what almost stopped them buying.
- Avoid leading questions and pitching.
- Record (with consent) and transcribe, so you can quote exact words.

Five to ten interviews with a defined group often reveal the main themes. That number is a practitioner rule of thumb, not a statistical guarantee.

### Jobs to be done

The *jobs to be done* (JTBD) idea, developed by Clayton Christensen and colleagues, holds that customers "hire" products to make progress in a particular circumstance. In their 2016 Harvard Business Review article, Christensen, Hall, Dillon and Duncan used the example of a milkshake bought by morning commuters to make a long, boring drive more interesting and to stay full until lunch. The competition for that job was not other milkshakes but bananas, bagels and boredom.

For copy, JTBD changes the question from "what is our product?" to "what progress is the reader trying to make, in what situation, and what else could they hire for it?" A job has three dimensions:

- **functional** (stay hydrated during long meetings),
- **emotional** (not feel embarrassed by a leaking bag),
- **social** (look organized and environmentally responsible to colleagues).

### Awareness levels

Eugene Schwartz (*Breakthrough Advertising*, 1966) described five stages of reader awareness. The right opening depends on the stage.

| Stage | What the reader knows | Where copy should start | Example opening for a time-tracking app |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unaware | Not aware of the problem | A story or fact that reveals the problem | "Freelancers lose about one billable hour a day without noticing." |
| Problem aware | Feels the problem, does not know solutions | The problem and its cost | "Ever finished a month and wondered where the hours went?" |
| Solution aware | Knows solutions exist, not yours | What a good solution must do | "Time tracking only works if you never have to remember to start it." |
| Product aware | Knows your product, not convinced | Proof, comparisons, objections | "Here is how it compares with the tool you use now." |
| Most aware | Knows and wants it | The offer | "Annual plan, 30% off until Friday." |

The billable hour figure is invented for the example. Schwartz also described market *sophistication*: in markets where buyers have seen many similar promises, a bigger claim no longer works, and copy must explain a new mechanism or approach instead.

### From research to copy

A *message map* turns research into decisions:

1. **Reader:** one sentence describing who and in what situation.
2. **Job:** the progress they want.
3. **Main promise:** the outcome, in their words.
4. **Proof points:** evidence for the promise.
5. **Top objections** with answers.
6. **Key phrases** to reuse verbatim.
7. **Awareness stage** and the opening that follows from it.

### Ethics and privacy

Use public reviews and your own customer data within the law. In the EU, using customer conversations for research is processing personal data under the GDPR and needs a legal basis and transparency. Quote customers in published copy only with their permission, and anonymize research material.

## Worked example

A small company sells an online course in spreadsheet skills for office workers.

**Review mining** of 120 reviews of three competing courses finds:

- Outcome phrases: "finally stopped being scared of formulas" (18 mentions in different words), "saved me hours every week" (15), "got the promotion" (4).
- Disappointments: "too fast for beginners" (22), "examples have nothing to do with my job" (14).
- Objections from course pages' questions: "Is it for my version of Excel?", "I am not a numbers person".

**Interviews** with six recent buyers show the trigger moment: a manager asked for a report and the person spent an evening on something "the analyst did in ten minutes".

**Message map.**

- Reader: office worker who uses spreadsheets daily but avoids formulas.
- Job: get routine reports done fast without asking for help, and stop feeling behind colleagues.
- Main promise: "Stop being scared of formulas. Build your weekly report in 15 minutes."
- Proof: before and after times from students (with permission), a free first lesson.
- Objections: version compatibility (answer: works in versions from 2016 and in Google Sheets), "not a numbers person" (answer: lessons use office examples, not math).
- Awareness: problem aware. Opening: the evening spent on a report.

All counts and claims are invented to show the method.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- **Depth versus speed.** A full research round takes days. For a small test, a few hours of review mining already improves copy considerably.
- **Representativeness.** Reviews and interviews come from those who speak up. Use them for language and themes, not for market sizes.
- **Research can stall a project.** Set a time limit and a question list.
- **Customers describe the past well and predict the future badly.** Ask what they did, not what they would do.

## Common mistakes

- **Researching only your own customers.** Fix: include competitors' reviews and people who did not buy.
- **Paraphrasing customer language into corporate language.** Fix: keep exact phrases in the copy where they fit.
- **Writing for the wrong awareness stage.** Fix: decide the stage before writing the opening.
- **Treating survey answers as purchase predictions.** Fix: use them for themes and words.

## Practice

1. Name five sources of customer insight and one limitation of each.
2. Explain the milkshake example and what it shows about competition.
3. For a product you know, write openings for three of Schwartz's awareness stages.
4. Mine 30 reviews of a product in a category you know and produce a message map.
