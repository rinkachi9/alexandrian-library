# Copywriting frameworks

## Summary

Copywriting frameworks are standard orders of argument that move a reader from attention to action: AIDA (attention, interest, desire, action), PAS (problem, agitate, solution), BAB (before, after, bridge), the 4Ps and the five step formula from the source notes. They are checklists distilled from practice, not scientific models, and none has been shown to beat the others in general. Their value is that they prevent common gaps, such as asking for action before the reader understands the offer. The right framework depends on the reader's awareness, the length of the piece and the price and risk of the decision.

## Core concepts

### Definition

A *copywriting framework* is a fixed sequence of persuasive moves used to structure a piece of copy. Each move has a job, and the order reflects a theory of how a reader decides. *Hierarchy of effects* models in marketing research make the same assumption: that people pass through stages from awareness to action.

*Analogy.* A framework is like the order of courses in a meal. Soup, main course, dessert is a convention that works for most meals and helps a cook plan, but a good cook changes it for a picnic, a tasting menu or a guest who is in a hurry. The analogy breaks because diners rarely leave after the soup, while most readers leave copy after the first lines, so the first move carries far more weight than the rest.

### The problem frameworks solve

Without structure, writers tend to start with what is most interesting to them (the company, the technology) and end without a clear request. Frameworks force the writer to start with the reader and end with an action, and to include the steps in between.

### AIDA

*AIDA* stands for Attention, Interest, Desire, Action.

1. **Attention.** Make the right reader notice (headline, image, first line).
2. **Interest.** Show that the message is relevant to them (their situation, a surprising fact).
3. **Desire.** Make them want the outcome (benefits, proof, picture of the result).
4. **Action.** Tell them what to do next and make it easy.

Edward K. Strong attributed the formula to the advertising practitioner E. St. Elmo Lewis in 1925 (*The Psychology of Selling and Advertising*), dating it to 1898. Recent historical research has questioned that attribution. As a research model, the strict sequence has been criticized: purchases do not always follow a linear path, and research on hierarchy of effects models found that the order of thinking, feeling and doing varies with the product and the buyer (Vakratsas and Ambler, 1999). As a writing checklist it remains useful.

### PAS

*PAS* stands for Problem, Agitate, Solution.

1. **Problem.** Name a problem the reader recognizes.
2. **Agitate.** Make the cost of the problem felt: consequences, frustration, what it is costing them.
3. **Solution.** Present the offer as the way out.

PAS suits problem-aware readers and short formats (ads, emails). The ethical limit is in step 2: agitating real costs is persuasion, inventing fears is manipulation and can be an aggressive commercial practice under EU law.

### BAB

*BAB* stands for Before, After, Bridge.

1. **Before.** The reader's current situation.
2. **After.** The situation once the problem is solved.
3. **Bridge.** How the offer gets them from before to after.

BAB is a compact form of the transformation step in the source notes and works well in social posts and case studies.

### The 4Ps

A common practitioner framework, attributed in various forms to direct mail copywriters, is *Promise, Picture, Proof, Push*:

1. **Promise** the main benefit.
2. **Picture** the reader enjoying it.
3. **Proof** that it is real.
4. **Push** to act now.

Another version uses Problem, Promise, Proof, Proposal. The origin is unclear, so treat the name as a convention.

### The five step formula from the source notes

The source notes, following a content marketing course, describe a formula for prompting action:

1. **Establish trust and authority.** Who you are and why the reader should listen.
2. **Describe the product or service.** Clear, concrete features.
3. **Emphasize benefits, emotional and logical.** How the reader will feel and why the choice makes sense.
4. **Paint a picture of the transformation.** Stories, case studies, before and after.
5. **Call to action** with risk reversal (see [calls to action and risk reversal](calls-to-action-and-risk-reversal.md)).

Compared with AIDA, it puts trust first, which suits readers who do not yet know the seller, for example in B2B or for expensive services.

### Choosing a framework

| Situation | Suitable framework | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short ad or social post, problem-aware reader | PAS or BAB | Fast, starts with what the reader feels |
| Product page for a familiar product | AIDA or 4Ps | Benefits and proof matter more than explaining the problem |
| Expensive or unfamiliar service, unknown seller | Five step formula | Trust must come before the offer |
| Long sales letter or landing page | Combination (see below) | Many objections to handle |
| Case study | BAB | The story is the proof |

### Anatomy of a long-form sales page

Long pages for considered purchases typically combine the frameworks in this order:

1. Headline and subheadline with the main promise.
2. Lead: the reader's situation or problem (PAS or a story).
3. Introduction of the solution and the unique mechanism (why it works when other things did not).
4. Benefits, each with proof.
5. Social proof: testimonials, case studies, numbers.
6. Details of the offer: what is included, price, bonuses.
7. Objection handling: FAQ, comparisons.
8. Risk reversal: guarantee.
9. Call to action, repeated at key points.
10. A short summary for skimmers who scroll to the end.

### Frameworks are not evidence

No controlled study shows that one framework beats the others across products and audiences. The research on hierarchy of effects models suggests that real decisions are more varied than any fixed sequence. Use frameworks to draft and to check for missing parts, then test the result with real readers.

## Worked example

The same offer, an online course in spreadsheet skills, written with three frameworks.

**PAS (email to problem-aware readers).**

> **Problem.** Another evening spent on a report the analyst would finish in ten minutes?
>
> **Agitate.** Every week it happens again. You copy numbers by hand, check them twice and still worry about a mistake in front of your manager.
>
> **Solution.** Our 30 lesson course shows you the six formulas that do 80% of office reporting, with examples from real office tasks. First lesson free.

**BAB (social post).**

> **Before:** two hours every Monday on the sales report.
> **After:** fifteen minutes, and the numbers update themselves.
> **Bridge:** lesson 12 of our course, "Lookup formulas without fear". Free preview below.

**Five step formula (landing page for HR managers buying for a team).**

1. Trust: "Built by a former finance trainer who taught 2,000 employees at three banks."
2. Product: 30 video lessons, exercises with real office files, a certificate.
3. Benefits: logical (hours saved, fewer errors), emotional (staff confidence, fewer requests to IT).
4. Transformation: a case study of a 12 person team that cut reporting time by half.
5. CTA and risk reversal: "Book a 20 minute demo. Pay only if the pilot group finishes the first module."

The "80%" and other figures are invented and would need evidence before publishing.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- **Speed versus originality.** Frameworks speed up drafting and reviewing. Used mechanically, they make copy sound like every other page.
- **Linear versus real decisions.** Readers jump around a page. Make each section work on its own, and repeat the call to action.
- **Agitation versus respect.** Strong agitation can raise response and damage trust. Keep it to real, documented costs.

## Common mistakes

- **Picking a framework before researching the reader.** Fix: decide the awareness stage first.
- **Skipping proof.** Fix: every framework needs evidence somewhere.
- **Exaggerated agitation.** Fix: describe real costs the reader recognizes.
- **Treating a framework as a guarantee.** Fix: test.

## Practice

1. Write the four stages of AIDA and the three of PAS and BAB.
2. Why might the five step formula suit B2B services better than AIDA?
3. What did research on hierarchy of effects models conclude about fixed sequences?
4. Write the same offer in PAS and BAB, and decide which suits a cold audience on social media better and why.
