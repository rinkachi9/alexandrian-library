# Persuasion principles

## Summary

Persuasion principles are regularities in how people respond to requests and offers: they follow what others do (social proof), value what seems scarce, are influenced by the first number they see (anchoring), and often weigh losses more than equal gains. Robert Cialdini's seven principles of influence are the best known summary. The research behind them is real, but many effects are smaller and more dependent on context than marketing books suggest, and some famous studies did not replicate. The same principles, used deceptively, become "dark patterns", which EU and US law increasingly prohibit.

## Core concepts

### Definition

A *persuasion principle* is a general tendency in human judgment that makes people more likely to say yes to a request under certain conditions. Most are *heuristics*: mental shortcuts that usually save effort and give good enough answers, and that can be triggered on purpose. A *cognitive bias* is the systematic error a heuristic produces when it is applied where it does not fit.

*Analogy.* Heuristics are like the autopilot in a car that keeps the lane. Most of the time it helps. A marketer who paints fake lane markings on the road can steer the car where they want. The analogy breaks because people, unlike autopilots, can notice the manipulation and lose trust in the whole road.

### Cialdini's principles of influence

Robert Cialdini described six principles in *Influence* (first edition 1984) and added a seventh, unity, in the expanded edition of 2021.

| Principle | Idea | Marketing example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reciprocity | People feel obliged to return a favor | A free, useful guide before an offer |
| Commitment and consistency | People act consistently with what they said or did before | A small first step (a free trial) before a purchase |
| Social proof | People look at what others do when unsure | Reviews, number of customers |
| Liking | People say yes to those they like or find similar | A friendly, recognizable voice |
| Authority | People defer to credible experts | Credentials, cited research |
| Scarcity | People value what is rare or disappearing | Limited stock, a real deadline |
| Unity | People favor those who share their identity | "For runners, by runners" |

### Social proof

*Social proof* is the tendency to use other people's behavior as evidence of what is correct, especially under uncertainty. In a field experiment in a hotel, Goldstein, Cialdini and Griskevicius (2008) found that a card saying most guests reused their towels led to more towel reuse than a card with a standard environmental message. A replication in two German hotels (Bohner and Schlüter, 2014) found that both kinds of message raised towel reuse compared with no message, but the descriptive norm message did not beat the standard environmental message. The general principle is well established, but the size of the effect of a single message varies a lot between settings.

Use in marketing: reviews and ratings, customer counts, case studies and testimonials. Fake reviews are illegal in the EU (since the 2019 Omnibus Directive amendments) and are prohibited by the US FTC rule on consumer reviews and testimonials (16 CFR Part 465), in effect since 21 October 2024.

### Scarcity

*Scarcity* is the tendency to value things more when they are rare or becoming unavailable. In a classic experiment, Worchel, Lee and Adewole (1975) found that cookies from a jar with few cookies were rated as more desirable than identical cookies from a jar with many. Cookies that became scarce were rated higher than those that were always scarce, and scarcity caused by demand from others raised ratings more than scarcity caused by an accident.

Use in marketing: real limited editions, real deadlines, real stock levels. A countdown timer that resets, or "only 2 left" when stock is not limited, is a deceptive practice. The source notes claim that urgency "increases dopamine levels". That is a loose reading of neuroscience and is not a basis for writing copy.

### Anchoring

*Anchoring* is the influence of an initial number on a later estimate, even when the number is irrelevant. Tversky and Kahneman (1974) described it in their work on heuristics and biases. In pricing, showing a premium option first makes the standard option look cheaper. The source notes describe this as presenting a high-value option first. The effect replicates well in laboratory studies, but its size depends on how plausible the anchor is and how much the buyer knows about prices.

### Loss aversion

*Loss aversion* is the tendency for a loss to feel larger than a gain of the same size. It is part of prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979). The source notes suggest framing benefits as avoiding a loss ("Don't miss out"). The evidence is debated: Gal and Rucker (2018) argued that loss aversion is much less general than textbooks claim, and Mrkva, Johnson, Gächter and Herrmann (2020) replied that it is real but depends on factors such as the size of the stakes. A fair summary is that loss framing can help, especially with larger stakes, but it is not a reliable two-to-one multiplier.

### Choice overload

The source advises against overloading the audience with information. A related idea, that too many options reduce buying, comes from the "jam study" of Iyengar and Lepper (2000). A meta-analysis of 50 experiments by Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd (2010) found a mean effect close to zero, with large differences between studies. Fewer options help in some conditions (complex products, unclear preferences) and not in others. Test rather than assume.

### Strength of evidence

| Claim | Status |
| --- | --- |
| People are influenced by what others do | Established |
| Scarcity raises perceived value | Established in laboratory studies, context dependent |
| Anchoring affects estimates and prices | Established, size varies |
| Losses loom larger than gains | Debated in strength and generality |
| Too many choices reduce purchases | Debated, average effect near zero |
| Urgency works by raising dopamine | Not supported as stated |
| People decide emotionally and justify logically | Simplification of research on emotion in decisions |

### Ethics and dark patterns

*Dark patterns* are interface designs that steer users into decisions they would not otherwise make, for example fake countdown timers, hidden costs, pre-ticked boxes or cancellation flows that are much harder than signing up. Mathur and colleagues (2019) crawled about 53,000 product pages from about 11,000 shopping sites and found 1,818 instances of dark patterns on 1,254 sites (about 11%), with more popular sites more likely to use them. In the EU, Article 25 of the Digital Services Act (Regulation 2022/2065) prohibits online platforms from designing interfaces that deceive or manipulate users, alongside the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive. In the US, the FTC treats many such designs as deceptive practices.

The source notes make the point that ethical persuasion informs and empowers people to make decisions that are right for them. A useful test: would the customer feel fine if they saw exactly how and why the page was designed this way?

## Worked example

An online course sells for 199 euros. The team wants more sign-ups.

Honest use of the principles:

- **Social proof:** show the real average rating (4.6 from 312 reviews) and two typical reviews, including one with a criticism.
- **Scarcity:** the live cohort has 40 seats because the instructor answers questions personally. Show the real number of seats left.
- **Anchoring:** show the "course plus personal review" option at 399 euros next to the 199 euro option.
- **Risk reversal:** a 30 day refund (see [calls to action and risk reversal](calls-to-action-and-risk-reversal.md)).

Deceptive versions to avoid: a timer that restarts when the page is reloaded, "only 3 seats left" shown to every visitor, a 399 euro "original price" for a course that was never sold at that price. In the EU, a price reduction must be shown against the lowest price of the previous 30 days (Price Indication Directive 98/6/EC as amended by Directive 2019/2161).

All numbers are invented for the example.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- **Short term versus long term.** Pressure raises conversions now and can raise refunds, complaints and churn later.
- **Effect sizes are context specific.** A principle that worked in one study or for one competitor may do nothing for you. Test it (see [A/B testing](ab-testing.md)).
- **Legal risk.** Fake scarcity, fake reviews and misleading reference prices are illegal in the EU and in the US.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a famous study as a law.** Fix: check replications and test in your own setting.
- **Fake urgency or scarcity.** Fix: use only real limits.
- **Social proof with small numbers.** Fix: show it only when the numbers help.
- **Stacking every principle on one page.** Fix: use the few that are true and relevant.
