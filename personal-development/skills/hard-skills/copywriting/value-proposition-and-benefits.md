# Value proposition and benefits

## Summary

A value proposition is the main reason a specific customer should choose your offer over the alternatives, including doing nothing. It is built from the customer's job, pains and desired gains, matched to what the product actually does, and it must be specific, credible and different. In copy, it is expressed through benefits (what the product does for the reader), supported by features (facts about the product) and proof (evidence that the benefit is real). Most weak product copy lists features, claims vague benefits or promises things it cannot prove.

## Prerequisites

[Customer research](customer-research.md) for the job, pains and language of the reader.

## Core concepts

### Definition

A *value proposition* is a statement of the specific benefit a defined customer gets from an offer, and why it is better for them than the alternatives. It answers three questions:

1. **For whom?** A defined reader in a situation.
2. **What outcome?** The benefit that matters to them.
3. **Why this and not the alternative?** The difference that justifies the choice.

A related older term is the *unique selling proposition* (USP), introduced by Rosser Reeves in *Reality in Advertising* (1961). Reeves required that an advertisement make a specific proposition to the buyer, that the proposition be one the competition does not or cannot offer, and that it be strong enough to move people to buy.

*Analogy.* A value proposition is like the answer to "why should I eat at your restaurant tonight?" asked by a specific person standing in a street with ten restaurants. "We have food" is true of all of them. "The only place open after midnight with a vegetarian menu" is an answer for a hungry vegetarian at 1 am. The analogy holds for specificity and comparison. It breaks because in a street the alternatives are visible, while online the reader may not know your competitors, so the alternative you must beat is often inaction.

### Features, benefits and outcomes

- A *feature* is a fact about the product: "450 W motor", "end-to-end encryption", "30 lessons".
- A *benefit* is what the feature does for the user: "crushes ice in 30 seconds", "nobody else can read your messages", "you learn at your own pace".
- An *outcome* is the change in the user's life that the benefit leads to: "smoothies for breakfast every day without lumps", "you can discuss a client case safely", "a promotion".

The *so what* technique moves from feature to outcome: state a feature, ask "so what?", answer, and repeat until the answer is something the reader cares about. *FAB* (features, advantages, benefits) is the sales training version of the same idea.

| Feature | So what? | Benefit | So what? | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Automatic time tracking runs in the background | You do not have to start a timer | No forgotten hours | Your invoices reflect the real work | About one more billable hour a day |
| Glass jar | Glass does not absorb smells or scratch like many plastics | The jar stays clean and clear | Your smoothie does not taste of yesterday's garlic soup | A kitchen tool you keep using |

Not every benefit should be pushed to an outcome. Big outcomes ("a promotion") are less credible and harder to prove than near benefits.

### The value proposition canvas

Osterwalder, Pigneur, Bernarda and Smith (*Value Proposition Design*, 2014) describe a canvas with two sides:

- **Customer profile:** *jobs* (what they try to get done), *pains* (bad outcomes, risks and obstacles), *gains* (outcomes and benefits they want).
- **Value map:** *products and services*, *pain relievers* (how the offer removes pains), *gain creators* (how it creates gains).

A *fit* exists when the pain relievers and gain creators address the most important jobs, pains and gains. The canvas is a planning tool. The copy then states the most important fit in plain words.

### Qualities of a strong value proposition

| Quality | Test | Weak | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Specific | Could the reader picture it? | "Save time" | "Build your weekly report in 15 minutes" |
| Relevant | Does it address a top job, pain or gain from research? | "Award-winning design" | "Fits any car cup holder" (top complaint about rivals) |
| Different | Could a competitor say the same? | "High quality" | "The only bottle with a two-year leak replacement" |
| Credible | Is there proof? | "The best course ever" | "Rated 4.7 by 1,240 students; first lesson free" |
| Clear | Is it understood in five seconds? | "Synergistic hydration solution" | "Cold water all day" |

### Kinds of proof

A promise without proof is a claim. Common kinds of proof, roughly from strongest to weakest in most contexts:

1. **Demonstration** the reader can check: a free trial, a sample, a live demo, a video of the test.
2. **Data with a method:** "below 10 °C after 24 hours in our test at 22 °C room temperature (method here)".
3. **Third-party evidence:** independent tests, certifications, press coverage.
4. **Customer evidence:** case studies with numbers, reviews, testimonials with full names (with permission).
5. **Credentials and experience** of the seller.
6. **Guarantees,** which do not prove the claim but show that the seller bears the risk if it is false (see [calls to action and risk reversal](calls-to-action-and-risk-reversal.md)).

Specific numbers are more believable than round ones ("1,240 reviews" rather than "over 1,000"), but only if true.

### Positioning against alternatives

Every value proposition is implicitly a comparison. The alternatives include:

- direct competitors (another bottle brand),
- different solutions to the same job (buying bottled water),
- doing it yourself,
- doing nothing.

Strong copy often names the alternative the reader actually uses and shows why the new option is better for their job. Comparative advertising is allowed in the EU under conditions set by Directive 2006/114/EC: it must compare like with like, objectively, and must not mislead or denigrate.

## Worked example

A bookkeeping service for freelance designers writes its value proposition.

**Research.** Interviews show that designers hate the end-of-quarter scramble for receipts, fear tax penalties and use either a general accountant who "does not understand freelancers" or do it themselves in spreadsheets.

**Canvas.**

- Job: stay compliant with taxes without losing design time.
- Pains: scramble for receipts, fear of penalties, accountants who ask confusing questions.
- Gains: knowing how much to set aside, a calm quarter end.
- Pain relievers: receipt upload from the phone, deadline reminders, an accountant who works only with creatives.
- Gain creators: a monthly "set aside this much" number.

**Draft value propositions.**

1. "Professional bookkeeping services for freelancers." Generic, no difference, no benefit.
2. "Bookkeeping that saves you time and money." Vague and unprovable as written.
3. "Bookkeeping for freelance designers: photograph receipts as you go, get a monthly 'set aside' number, and never scramble at quarter end again." Specific reader, specific mechanism, clear outcome.

**Proof to add.** "Used by 340 designers since 2022. Average time spent on bookkeeping: 20 minutes a month (client survey, 2026)." Numbers invented for the example and would need a real source.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- **Narrow versus broad.** A narrow value proposition (for freelance designers) converts the target group better but excludes others. A broad one reaches more people less effectively. Many businesses use different pages for different segments.
- **Bold versus credible.** A bigger promise attracts attention but needs stronger proof. If proof is weak, a smaller, specific promise usually performs better.
- **Emotional versus functional benefits.** Both matter. Functional benefits justify, emotional ones motivate. Research tells you which the reader emphasizes.
- **Comparison.** Naming competitors can clarify the difference but invites legal and reputational risk. Compare fairly and verifiably.

## Common mistakes

- **Features without benefits.** Fix: apply "so what?" to each feature.
- **Benefits without proof.** Fix: attach evidence to every important claim.
- **A value proposition that any competitor could use.** Fix: run the "could a competitor say this?" test.
- **Writing for everyone.** Fix: name the reader and their situation.
- **Ignoring "do nothing" as the main competitor.** Fix: show the cost of the status quo.
