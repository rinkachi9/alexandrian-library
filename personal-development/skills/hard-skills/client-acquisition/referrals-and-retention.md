# Referrals and retention

## Summary

Keeping clients and getting recommendations from them are usually the cheapest and most reliable ways to grow a service business. Retention depends on delivering what was promised, communicating well and continuing to add value, and it is measured by churn, repeat purchase and revenue retention. Referrals come mostly from satisfied clients who are asked at the right moment and given an easy way to recommend. Popular claims such as "a 5% increase in retention raises profits by 25% to 95%" come from a 1990 article whose evidence has been questioned, and the Net Promoter Score, widely used to predict growth, has not consistently outperformed other satisfaction measures in independent tests. The principles hold, the famous numbers should be used with care.

## Core concepts

### Definitions

- *Retention* is keeping existing clients buying over time. Its opposite is *churn* (the share of clients lost in a period).
- *Revenue retention* measures how much revenue from existing clients is kept over a period, including expansions and reductions.
- A *referral* is a recommendation of your business by a client or contact to someone who might need it.
- *Word of mouth* is the broader, often unprompted spread of opinions between people.

*Analogy.* A client base is like a bucket you fill with water. Acquisition pours water in, churn is the hole at the bottom. Pouring faster while ignoring the hole is expensive and tiring. Fixing the hole lets the same pouring fill the bucket, and satisfied clients who refer others are like a second tap. The analogy breaks because water is all the same, while clients differ greatly in value, so keeping the right clients matters more than keeping all of them.

### Evidence and its limits

- **Reichheld and Sasser (1990),** "Zero defections: Quality comes to services" (Harvard Business Review), argued that reducing customer defections by 5% could raise profits by 25% to 85% in the service businesses they examined, because long-term customers buy more, cost less to serve and refer others. The figures are widely repeated, often as "25% to 95%". Critics, such as Byron Sharp, have argued that the article did not show empirically that lower defection causes such profit increases, and Reinartz and Kumar (2000) found that long-lived customers are not always the most profitable.
- **Net Promoter Score (NPS),** introduced by Reichheld in 2003, asks how likely a customer is to recommend the company on a scale of 0 to 10, and subtracts the share of detractors (0 to 6) from the share of promoters (9 to 10). Keiningham and colleagues (2007) tried to replicate the claim that NPS is the single best predictor of growth using Norwegian data and did not find it superior to other satisfaction measures. NPS is useful as a simple tracking question and as a prompt for follow-up, not as a proven predictor.
- **Referred customers:** a study of a German bank by Schmitt, Skiera and Van den Bulte (2011) found that customers acquired through a referral program were more loyal and more valuable over the period studied than other customers. One study in one industry, but consistent with practice.

### Drivers of retention

1. **Results:** the client achieves the outcome they paid for.
2. **Expectations:** the scope, timeline and outcomes were described accurately in the proposal (see [proposals and pricing](proposals-and-pricing.md)).
3. **Communication:** regular updates, quick responses, early warning of problems.
4. **Relationship:** knowing the client's goals and people.
5. **Continued value:** new ideas, proactive suggestions, sharing relevant information.
6. **Handling problems:** fast, fair recovery when something goes wrong.

### Onboarding

The first weeks set the tone. A clear onboarding plan (kick-off meeting, shared goals and metrics, contacts, schedule, first quick win) reduces early churn and misunderstandings.

### Measuring retention

- **Client churn rate** = clients lost in a period / clients at the start of the period.
- **Repeat rate** = share of clients who buy again within a period.
- **Revenue retention** = revenue from clients who existed at the start of a period, at the end of it, divided by their revenue at the start.
- **Satisfaction:** short surveys after milestones, NPS or customer satisfaction questions, with follow-up conversations.
- **Early warning signs:** slower replies, missed meetings, reduced usage, new contacts on the client side, complaints.

### Getting referrals

- **Deliver first:** referrals follow results.
- **Ask at the right moment:** after a success, a positive comment or a completed milestone.
- **Make it specific:** "Do you know another e-commerce manager who is planning a platform migration this year?" works better than "Do you know anyone who might need us?"
- **Make it easy:** offer a short text or a link they can forward, or ask for a direct introduction.
- **Thank and update:** tell the referrer what happened. Some businesses offer a gift or referral fee. In regulated professions and in some contexts, referral payments are restricted or must be disclosed.
- **Testimonials and case studies:** ask satisfied clients for permission to publish their story (see [storytelling](../content-marketing/storytelling.md)).

### Expansion

*Expansion* (upselling and cross-selling) means existing clients buy more: a larger scope, additional services, a longer contract. It is often easier than acquiring new clients because trust exists, as long as the additional offer serves the client's goals.

## Worked example

A small marketing agency with 30 retainer clients reviews a year (invented numbers).

- **Churn:** 9 clients left (30% churn). Exit conversations show: 4 felt reports did not show business results, 3 had budget cuts, 2 had a change of contact person who brought another agency.
- **Actions:**
  1. Reports restructured around each client's business goals (leads, sales), not activity.
  2. Quarterly strategy meetings with the decision maker, not only the day-to-day contact.
  3. When a client's contact person changes, a meeting with the new person within two weeks.
  4. After each successful quarter, a specific referral request.
- **Next year:** churn falls to 5 clients (17%). Referrals bring 6 new clients, compared with 2 the year before.

The improvement cannot be attributed with certainty to each action, and market conditions may have changed. The agency tracks the warning signs for each client monthly.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- **Retention versus acquisition effort.** Both are needed. Most small firms underinvest in retention.
- **Keeping every client** is not the goal. Some clients cost more than they bring. Use your ideal customer profile.
- **Referral incentives** can increase referrals and can make recommendations look less genuine. Consider the context and rules.
- **Surveys** give signals. Conversations give reasons.

## Common mistakes

- **Silence between deliverables.** Fix: regular, results-focused communication.
- **Ignoring early warning signs.** Fix: monitor and act early.
- **Never asking for referrals.** Fix: ask specifically after successes.
- **Quoting the "5% retention, 25% to 95% profit" figure as fact.** Fix: describe it as a 1990 claim with contested evidence.
- **Treating NPS as a proven growth predictor.** Fix: use it as one signal and follow up with conversations.

## Further reading

- Reichheld, F. F., & Sasser, W. E. (1990). Zero defections: Quality comes to services. *Harvard Business Review*, 68(5), 105-111. The classic argument for retention.
- Reichheld, F. F. (2003). The one number you need to grow. *Harvard Business Review*, 81(12), 46-54. The Net Promoter Score.
- Keiningham, T. L., Cooil, B., Andreassen, T. W., & Aksoy, L. (2007). A longitudinal examination of net promoter and firm revenue growth. *Journal of Marketing*, 71(3), 39-51. [doi:10.1509/jmkg.71.3.39](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.71.3.39). A replication that did not support NPS superiority.
- Reinartz, W. J., & Kumar, V. (2000). On the profitability of long-life customers in a noncontractual setting. *Journal of Marketing*, 64(4), 17-35. [doi:10.1509/jmkg.64.4.17.18077](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.64.4.17.18077). Long-lived customers are not always the most profitable.
- Schmitt, P., Skiera, B., & Van den Bulte, C. (2011). Referral programs and customer value. *Journal of Marketing*, 75(1), 46-59. [doi:10.1509/jmkg.75.1.46](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.75.1.46). Value of referred customers.
