# Outreach and prospecting

## Summary

Prospecting is finding and researching potential clients who match your ideal customer profile, and outreach is contacting them first, by email, phone, social messages or in person. Done well, it is a relevant, personal message to a well-chosen person about a problem they probably have, with a small, easy next step. Done badly, it is spam that damages reputation and, in many countries, breaks the law. In the EU, unsolicited electronic marketing generally requires prior consent, and in Poland since November 2024 commentators read the rule as covering business recipients as well, which makes cold email and cold calling legally risky there. Warm outreach through referrals, shared contexts and prior engagement is both more effective and safer.

## Core concepts

### Definitions

- *Prospecting:* identifying and researching organizations and people who fit the ICP and might need your offer.
- *Outreach:* the first contact you initiate, and the follow-ups.
- *Cold outreach:* contact with someone who has no prior relationship with you.
- *Warm outreach:* contact with some prior connection: a mutual contact, an event, an interaction with your content, a referral.

*Analogy.* Outreach is like knocking on doors in a neighborhood. Knocking on every door with the same sales pitch annoys people, gets doors closed and may get you reported. Knocking on the few doors where you know the owner has just mentioned a leaking roof, with a neighbor's introduction and a useful tip, starts a conversation. The analogy breaks because an email or message can be sent to thousands of "doors" at no cost, which is why both recipients and the law treat unsolicited messages much more strictly than a knock on a door.

### Law and consent

**EU.** Article 13 of the ePrivacy Directive (2002/58/EC) requires prior consent for unsolicited communications for direct marketing by email and automated calling to natural persons, and leaves member states to decide on protection for legal persons. National laws differ. Prospect data is personal data under the GDPR: processing needs a legal basis (often legitimate interest for B2B research), the person must be informed (Article 14 when data was not collected from them), and they can object.

**Poland.** Since 10 November 2024, Article 398 of the Electronic Communications Law (Prawo komunikacji elektronicznej) requires prior consent for direct marketing using electronic communications and automated calling systems. Legal commentators read it as applying to business recipients too and as excluding cold email or cold calling that presents an offer without prior consent. Contacting someone to ask for permission to send an offer may itself be treated as marketing. Check current interpretation and guidance before running outreach in Poland.

**US.** The CAN-SPAM Act allows commercial email without prior consent, but requires accurate sender information, a non-deceptive subject, a physical postal address and a working opt-out honored within 10 business days. Telephone outreach is restricted by the Telephone Consumer Protection Act and the Do Not Call registry.

This note is educational and is not legal advice.

### Prospecting research

For each prospect, find:

- **Fit:** does the organization match the ICP?
- **Trigger:** a recent event that creates a need (hiring, funding, expansion, new regulation, a public problem).
- **Person:** who feels the problem and who decides.
- **Context:** something specific to refer to (a post, an interview, a product change).
- **Path:** a warm route if one exists (mutual contact, shared group, event).

Sources: company websites, job postings, news, LinkedIn (see [LinkedIn for professionals](../social-media/linkedin-for-professionals.md)), public registers, industry directories, your network.

### Warm paths first

In order of warmth and typical effectiveness:

1. Referral or introduction by a mutual contact.
2. Prior interaction (they downloaded your content, attended your webinar, commented on your post).
3. Shared context (same event, community, alumni group).
4. Engaging publicly first (thoughtful comments on their posts) before a private message.
5. Fully cold contact.

### Writing an outreach message

A structure that respects the recipient:

1. **Relevance:** why them, why now (the trigger or context).
2. **Problem or insight:** something specific and useful about their situation.
3. **Credibility:** one line of proof (a similar client, a result).
4. **Small ask:** an easy next step (a short call, a resource, permission to send something).
5. **Easy exit:** make it simple to say no.

Keep it short, personal and free of hype. Avoid fake familiarity ("Hope you're well" from a stranger), fake urgency and misleading subject lines.

> Subject: Checkout drop on your new mobile site
>
> Hello Tomasz, I saw you moved the shop to a new platform last month. I tested the mobile checkout and the delivery step reloads the page when the postcode is entered, which loses the address. In a similar migration for [client], fixing that kind of issue raised completed checkouts noticeably.
>
> If useful, I can send a two minute screen recording of what I found. No obligation.

The client and result must be real and shareable. Note the legal context: in Poland, sending this without prior consent may be unlawful, so a warm path (introduction, prior interaction) or a different channel may be needed.

### Follow-up

Many replies come after a follow-up. A sequence of two or three follow-ups over two to three weeks, each adding something useful (a resource, a relevant example) rather than "just checking in", is common practice. Stop when the person asks or does not respond after the sequence. Honor opt-outs immediately.

### Volume and quality

Personalized outreach to a small number of well-researched prospects usually produces better results per hour and less reputational damage than mass sequences. Mass automated outreach also harms email deliverability (see [email marketing](../content-marketing/email-marketing.md#deliverability)) and may break platform terms when automated on LinkedIn.

### Tracking

A simple pipeline tracker (spreadsheet or CRM) with: prospect, fit, trigger, date of each touch, response, next step. Metrics: response rate, positive response rate, meetings booked, and eventually clients won per prospects contacted.

## Worked example

A UX consultant plans outreach to e-commerce companies in Germany (invented numbers).

1. **List:** 40 shops that changed platforms or redesigned in the last six months (found through news, job postings and site changes).
2. **Research:** for each, the head of e-commerce and a specific observation from testing their site.
3. **Legal check:** German law (§ 7 UWG, the Act against Unfair Competition) treats email advertising without prior express consent as an unacceptable nuisance, including toward businesses. The consultant therefore does not cold-email.
4. **Warm approach:** comments substantively on prospects' LinkedIn posts for two weeks, attends an e-commerce meetup in Berlin, and asks two existing clients for introductions.
5. **Outreach:** after an interaction, a personal LinkedIn message referring to it, with one specific observation and an offer to share the recording.
6. **Results:** 14 conversations, 6 calls, 2 projects.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- **Cold versus warm.** Cold outreach can reach anyone and is legally risky in parts of the EU and less effective. Warm outreach takes longer to set up and converts better.
- **Personalization versus scale.** Each degree of personalization costs time and increases response.
- **Persistence versus annoyance.** A few useful follow-ups are expected. Many generic ones damage reputation.
- **Channel choice.** Email, phone and social messages have different rules and norms by country and audience.

## Common mistakes

- **Ignoring the law.** Fix: check consent rules for each country and channel.
- **Generic templates.** Fix: research and refer to something specific.
- **Asking for too much.** Fix: a small next step.
- **No follow-up, or endless follow-up.** Fix: a short, planned sequence.
- **Buying lists.** Fix: build your own researched list. Bought lists are often outdated, illegal to use and harmful to deliverability.
