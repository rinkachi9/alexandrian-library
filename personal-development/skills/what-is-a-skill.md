# What is a skill

## Summary

A skill is a learned ability to carry out a task to a standard, reliably and repeatedly. It differs from knowledge (knowing that something is true), from ability (a relatively stable capacity such as reasoning speed) and from competence (the proven use of knowledge, skills and attitudes in a real situation with responsibility). Skills are usually sorted into hard skills, tied to a task or a tool and easy to test, and soft skills, which transfer across roles and are harder to measure. The distinction is useful for planning learning but is a convention, not a law of nature, and most real work needs both at once.

## Core concepts

### Definition

A *skill* is the ability to perform a task, acquired through learning and practice, that shows itself in performance that meets a standard and can be repeated. Three parts of the definition matter:

- **Learned.** A skill is acquired. Height or a fast reaction time help in some tasks, but they are not skills.
- **Task.** A skill is always a skill *at* something: writing a product description, negotiating a price, running a regression.
- **Standard and repeatability.** Doing something well once by luck is not a skill. A skill shows up as performance that is reliably good enough under normal conditions.

The European Qualifications Framework (EQF, Council Recommendation of 22 May 2017) defines skills as "the ability to apply knowledge and use know-how to complete tasks and solve problems", and distinguishes *cognitive* skills (logical, intuitive and creative thinking) from *practical* skills (manual dexterity and the use of methods, materials, tools and instruments).

*Analogy.* A skill is like a well-worn path through a field. The first time you cross, you pick your way slowly and make mistakes. After many crossings the path is clear, you walk it quickly without thinking, and you can walk it at night. The path exists only because it was walked: it is learned, it leads somewhere specific, and it is reliable. The analogy breaks in two places. A path in a field cannot be widened to a new field, while some skills partly transfer to new tasks. And an unused path grows over slowly and evenly, while unused skills decay unevenly: well-practiced motor skills survive years of disuse much better than detailed procedural knowledge.

### Knowledge, skills, abilities and competences

Occupational research and education policy use four related terms. They are often mixed up, so it pays to keep them apart.

| Term | Meaning | Example for a copywriter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Knowledge | Facts, concepts and principles in a domain ("knowing that") | Knowing what the EU rules on misleading advertising forbid |
| Skill | The learned ability to perform a task to a standard ("knowing how") | Turning product features into clear benefits in two sentences |
| Ability | A relatively stable capacity that helps learn and perform many tasks | Verbal fluency, working memory |
| Competence | The proven use of knowledge, skills and personal attitudes in a real context, with responsibility and autonomy | Delivering a sales page on deadline that converts and passes legal review |

The O*NET database of the US Department of Labor, which describes over 900 occupations, keeps these apart in its content model. It separates *knowledge* (organized sets of principles and facts), *abilities* (enduring attributes of the person) and *skills*, which it divides into *basic skills* (developed capacities that make learning easier, such as reading comprehension, writing and active learning) and *cross-functional skills* (capacities that help perform activities that occur across jobs, such as complex problem solving and social skills). The EU's ESCO classification (European Skills, Competences, Qualifications and Occupations) does the same for European labor markets. Its version 1.2, released in May 2024, describes thousands of skills and knowledge concepts linked to occupations.

The practical consequence: when you plan to "learn SEO", separate what you need to *know* (how search engines rank pages) from what you need to *be able to do* (run a keyword analysis, fix a crawling problem). Knowledge can be read. Skill has to be practiced.

### Declarative and procedural knowledge

Cognitive psychology describes the same difference with two terms. *Declarative knowledge* is knowledge you can state ("a canonical tag tells search engines which URL is the main version"). *Procedural knowledge* is knowledge of how to do something, often expressed only in performance ("noticing at a glance that a site has a duplicate content problem"). John Anderson's ACT theory (1982) describes skill learning as the conversion of declarative into procedural knowledge through practice (see [skill acquisition](skill-acquisition.md)).

Part of every skill is *tacit*: the philosopher Michael Polanyi (1966) summarized it as "we can know more than we can tell". An experienced editor senses that a sentence is weak before they can say why. Tacit knowledge is the reason books alone rarely make anyone skilled, and why feedback from a skilled person speeds up learning.

### Hard and soft skills

The most common division in everyday use is between hard and soft skills.

- *Hard skills* are tied to a specific task, tool or field, can be taught in a structured way, and can be tested with a clear standard: touch typing, writing SQL queries, setting up a Google Ads campaign, bookkeeping.
- *Soft skills* are transferable skills that concern how a person thinks, works with others and manages themselves: communication, teamwork, self-regulation, problem solving.

The term "soft skills" comes from US Army training research. At a 1972 conference of the US Continental Army Command (CONARC), soft skills were tentatively defined as job-related skills that involve little or no interaction with machines and whose application on the job is quite generalized. The same participants noted the limits of the hard and soft labels. Today the US National Research Council (2012) prefers to group transferable competencies into three domains: *cognitive*, *intrapersonal* and *interpersonal*.

The distinction is useful because the two kinds are learned and measured differently (see [hard skills fundamentals](hard-skills/hard-skills-fundamentals.md) and [soft skills fundamentals](soft-skills/soft-skills-fundamentals.md)). It misleads when it suggests that soft skills are less important or less rigorous, or that the two can be separated in real work. Writing a sales email is a hard skill (structure, persuasion techniques) performed through soft skills (understanding the reader, managing your own impatience).

### Skill levels

Skills are not on or off. Several models describe levels:

- The **Dreyfus model** (Dreyfus and Dreyfus, 1980 and 1986) describes five stages: novice, advanced beginner, competent, proficient and expert. A novice follows context-free rules. An expert perceives the situation as a whole and acts intuitively, falling back on analysis only when something is unusual.
- **Frameworks of qualifications** such as the EQF describe eight levels of learning outcomes by knowledge, skills and responsibility and autonomy.
- **Practical rubrics** in companies describe what a junior, mid-level and senior person can do without help.

A simple, usable scale for self-assessment is the one used in [skills inventory and gap analysis](../career/self-assessment/skills-inventory-and-gap-analysis.md): aware, can do with help, can do alone, can teach others, each backed by evidence.

### Transfer

*Transfer* is the use of a skill learned in one context in another. *Near transfer* (from one spreadsheet program to another) is common. *Far transfer* (from chess to mathematics, or from music lessons to general intelligence) is rare: meta-analyses of chess instruction, music instruction and working memory training found small effects that shrank as the quality of the studies improved (Sala and Gobet, 2017). The practical lesson is to practice the skill you actually need, in conditions close to those in which you will use it, and not to expect a general "brain training" effect.

### How skills change in value

The labor market value of a skill changes over time. The World Economic Forum's *Future of Jobs Report 2025*, based on a survey of employers, reports that employers expect 39% of workers' core skills to change by 2030. Deming and Noray (2020) showed with online job postings that the skill content of technology-intensive jobs changes fast, and that the earnings advantage of graduates in fields such as computer science is largest at entry and shrinks as their skills become obsolete. Skills tied to specific tools age faster than foundational skills such as writing, quantitative reasoning or working with people.

## Worked example

Breaking down "being good at e-commerce SEO" for a shop owner who wants to learn it.

| Component | Type | How to acquire | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| How search engines crawl, index and rank | Knowledge | Read [how search engines work](hard-skills/search-engine-optimization/how-search-engines-work.md) | Explain it to someone without notes |
| Running a keyword analysis for a category | Hard skill | Do 10 analyses, compare with an expert's | The chosen keywords match the intent of the top results |
| Writing an original product description | Hard skill | Write, get feedback, rewrite | A reviewer cannot tell which supplier text it came from |
| Noticing a duplicate content problem on sight | Tacit, procedural | Audit many sites, discuss findings with an experienced person | Correctly flags problems in a test site |
| Sticking with a slow, uncertain project for months | Intrapersonal soft skill | Set weekly goals and review them | The work continues after the first disappointing month |
| Persuading a developer to fix a technical issue | Interpersonal soft skill | Prepare a short business case | The issue is fixed |

The breakdown shows that one "skill" in a job advertisement is in fact a bundle of knowledge, several hard skills, tacit judgment and soft skills. Planning learning at this level is more useful than "learning SEO".

## Trade offs and when to use it

- **Fine-grained versus broad skill names.** A list of 40 micro-skills is precise but hard to use. "SEO" is easy to use but hides what you can and cannot do. Use broad names for communication (a resume) and fine-grained ones for learning plans.
- **Hard and soft labels.** Useful for planning, harmful when they lead you to neglect one kind. Treat them as two lenses on the same work.
- **Frameworks.** O*NET, ESCO and the EQF give a shared vocabulary and are useful for comparing jobs and qualifications. They are slow to change and describe average occupations, not your specific role.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing knowing with being able to do.** Fix: for every learning goal, define a task you will be able to perform and how you will check it.
- **Counting hours instead of performance.** Fix: measure what you can do, not how long you studied (see [deliberate practice](deliberate-practice.md)).
- **Expecting far transfer.** Fix: practice the target skill in realistic conditions.
- **Treating soft skills as personality.** Many soft skills can be developed. Fix: describe them as behaviors you can practice.
