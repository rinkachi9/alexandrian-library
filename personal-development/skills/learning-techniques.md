# Learning techniques

## Summary

Some study and practice methods produce much more durable learning than others, and the ones people use most are often among the weakest. A large review by Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated practice testing (retrieving information from memory) and distributed practice (spacing study over time) as the most effective of ten common techniques, and rereading and highlighting as among the least effective. Effective techniques often feel harder and slower during study, a pattern called desirable difficulties. This note covers the techniques with the strongest evidence and how to apply them to learning a skill, not only facts.

## Core concepts

### Definition

A *learning technique* is a method a learner uses on their own to acquire, retain and later use knowledge or skill: rereading, testing oneself, explaining material, spacing sessions, mixing problem types. Techniques differ from teaching methods because the learner controls them.

*Analogy.* Memory is less like a recording and more like a path in a forest that has to be walked to stay open. Rereading is looking at a map of the path: it feels like progress, but your feet learn nothing. Retrieval practice is walking the path from memory, stumbling and finding your way. Spacing is walking it again just before it grows over. The analogy breaks because, unlike a path, retrieving a memory also changes it, sometimes adding errors, so feedback after retrieval matters.

### Desirable difficulties

Robert Bjork (1994) introduced the term *desirable difficulties* for conditions that slow down apparent progress during practice but improve long-term retention and transfer: spacing, interleaving, testing and varying conditions. The reason is that learning is strengthened by the effort of reconstructing and retrieving, not by easy re-exposure. The practical problem is that learners judge their learning by how easy study feels, so they prefer the less effective methods. Fluency during study is a poor signal of learning.

### Ratings from the Dunlosky review

Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan and Willingham (2013) reviewed the evidence for ten techniques across learners, materials and tasks, and rated their utility:

| Utility | Technique | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High | Practice testing | Retrieving from memory: flashcards, practice questions, explaining without notes |
| High | Distributed practice | Spreading study over several sessions instead of cramming |
| Moderate | Elaborative interrogation | Asking "why is this true?" and answering it |
| Moderate | Self-explanation | Explaining how new information relates to what you know, or explaining steps while solving |
| Moderate | Interleaved practice | Mixing different kinds of problems in one session |
| Low | Summarization | Writing summaries of texts |
| Low | Highlighting and underlining | Marking important passages |
| Low | Keyword mnemonic | Linking a word to a vivid image |
| Low | Imagery for text | Forming mental images while reading |
| Low | Rereading | Reading the material again |

"Low" does not mean useless. It means the evidence for benefit is weak, limited to some conditions, or the gains are small compared with the time spent.

### Retrieval practice

*Retrieval practice* means bringing information or a procedure back from memory instead of re-reading it. In an influential experiment, Roediger and Karpicke (2006) had students study prose passages. Students who studied once and then took recall tests forgot less after one week than students who studied the passage repeatedly, although the repeated-study group did better on a test five minutes later. That reversal is the testing effect in a nutshell: the method that feels better now is worse later.

For skills, retrieval means performing from memory: writing a sales email without the template, auditing a page without the checklist, then comparing with the reference.

### Distributed practice

The *spacing effect* is the finding that the same amount of study produces better long-term retention when spread over time. A meta-analysis by Cepeda and colleagues (2006) of more than 300 experiments confirmed it across materials and ages. A later study (Cepeda et al., 2008) suggested that the best gap between sessions grows with how long you want to remember: for retention over months, gaps of days to weeks work better than gaps of hours. A practical rule is to review new material after one day, then after several days, then after a few weeks.

### Interleaving

*Interleaved practice* mixes different problem types or skills within a session (ABCABC), instead of practicing one type in a block (AAABBBCCC). It is harder, because each problem requires first recognizing what kind of problem it is, but that recognition is part of the real skill. In real work nobody tells you that the next email needs a price objection answer. Interleaving helps most when the problem types are similar enough to be confused.

### Elaboration and self-explanation

*Elaborative interrogation* asks why a fact is true. *Self-explanation* asks how a step follows from the previous one or from principles. Both link new material to existing knowledge, which makes it easier to retrieve and apply. For skills, a useful form is the after-action review: after a task, explain what you did, why, what happened and what you would change.

### Worked examples and the expertise reversal effect

For beginners, studying *worked examples* (complete solutions with steps) is more efficient than solving problems from scratch, because it reduces the load on working memory. This is the *worked example effect* from cognitive load theory (Sweller and colleagues). As learners gain expertise, the benefit disappears and can reverse: experts learn more from solving problems than from studying solutions they already understand (the *expertise reversal effect*, Kalyuga et al., 2003). Start with examples, then fade them out.

### Sleep and rest

Sleep after learning supports the consolidation of memories, both of facts and of motor skills. Studying through the night before a test trades long-term learning for short-term performance. This note does not cover the mechanisms, which belong to neuroscience.

## Worked example

A plan to learn [on-page SEO](hard-skills/search-engine-optimization/on-page-seo.md) in four weeks, three sessions per week of 45 minutes.

| Week | Session focus | Technique |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Read the note once. Then, without notes, write the elements of on-page SEO and why each matters. Check against the note. | Retrieval, self-explanation |
| 1 | Study three fully annotated example pages (good and bad). | Worked examples |
| 2 | Audit two real pages with the checklist, then one without it. | Fading worked examples, retrieval |
| 2 | Review week 1 questions from memory. | Spacing |
| 3 | Audit a mixed set: a product page, a blog post and a category page in one session. | Interleaving |
| 3 | Answer "why" for each recommendation in your audit. | Elaborative interrogation |
| 4 | Audit two new pages without help, compare with an expert's audit, note differences. | Retrieval with feedback |
| 4 | Review all earlier questions from memory. | Spacing |

Notice what the plan avoids: rereading the note several times, highlighting it, or practicing only product pages for a week.

## Trade offs and when to use it

- **Effective methods feel worse.** Expect slower apparent progress and more errors. Judge by delayed tests, not by how study feels.
- **Testing needs good questions.** Questions that only check recognition ("Which of these is a title tag?") produce weaker learning than questions that require recall or application.
- **Spacing needs planning.** It does not fit a last-minute deadline. For a test tomorrow, cramming helps tomorrow and fails next month.
- **Most evidence comes from education settings** with students and text material. Applying it to workplace skills is a reasonable extension, but less directly tested.

## Common mistakes

- **Rereading as the main method.** Fix: close the book and retrieve.
- **Highlighting instead of thinking.** Fix: turn highlights into questions and answer them later.
- **Blocking practice by topic.** Fix: mix problem types once the basics are clear.
- **Studying solutions after you are past the beginner stage.** Fix: try first, then look at the solution.
- **Judging learning by fluency.** Fix: test yourself after a delay.
