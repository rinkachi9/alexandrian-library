# Storytelling

## Summary

A story is an account of events with characters, a goal, an obstacle and a change. People remember and are moved by stories more than by lists of facts, because stories give structure, emotion and a sense of cause. Green and Brock (2000) showed that readers who were absorbed in a story, a state they called transportation, held beliefs more in line with the story. The effect has been replicated in many studies, and the evidence for the mechanism is more mixed. In business and leadership, stories are used to explain, persuade and build culture.

## Core concepts

### Definition

*Storytelling* is the use of narrative to communicate. A story differs from a report in that it follows someone who wants something, meets an obstacle and ends changed. *Narrative transportation* is the state of being absorbed in a story, with imagery, emotion and focused attention (Green and Brock, 2000).

### Analogy

A story is like a map that also shows the journey: the facts are the roads, and the plot is the route that a traveler took. The limit of the analogy is that a story chooses what to show and leaves out the rest, so a vivid story can mislead.

### Components

| Component | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Character | Someone the audience can care about |
| Goal | What the character wants |
| Obstacle | What stands in the way: conflict creates tension |
| Stakes | What is lost if the goal fails |
| Turning point | A choice or event that changes direction |
| Resolution | Outcome and what was learned |

### The classic arc

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  S[Setup: character and world] --> I[Inciting event]
  I --> R[Rising action: obstacles]
  R --> C[Climax: decisive moment]
  C --> F[Resolution: result and meaning]
```

### Why stories work

- **Memory.** A narrative links facts through cause and sequence, which aids recall.
- **Emotion.** Emotion directs attention and strengthens memory.
- **Identification.** Audiences see through the character's eyes, which lowers counter-arguing in the study of Green and Brock (2000): highly transported readers noticed fewer flaws in the story and held more story-consistent beliefs.

Later work finds that the manipulation of transportation did not always succeed, and that some studies do not find that transportation mediates persuasion, so the effect is better established than its explanation.

### Types

Personal stories (to build rapport), case stories (to explain an idea), origin stories (to explain why a group exists), vision stories (to describe a future) and teaching stories (parables). Match the type to the purpose.

### Developing the skill

| Practice | How |
| --- | --- |
| Collect stories | Keep a notebook of events with a clear turn |
| Tell and test | Tell a story to one person, watch where attention drops |
| Cut | Remove details that do not serve the point |
| Use concrete detail | One specific detail beats a general description |
| Know the point | Each story supports one message |

### Pitfalls

A story that is too long, has no point, or exaggerates loses trust. Do not fabricate. A story is evidence of one case, and does not prove a general claim.

## Worked example

A manager wants the team to accept a stricter testing rule.

| Version | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Statement | "Testing must be done before release" |
| Story | "Last spring Priya shipped a small fix without the full test run. At 2 a.m. the billing page failed for 800 customers. We spent the weekend repairing it. A 20-minute test would have caught it." |

The story has a character, a goal, an obstacle and stakes, and it ties the rule to a consequence. The manager follows with the data: the share of failed releases before and after the rule.

## Trade offs and when to use it

Stories are effective for persuading and teaching, and for organizational culture. They can manipulate when emotional detail replaces evidence. Combine a story with data, and be honest about how typical the case is.

## Common mistakes

- **No point.** Tell it for a reason.
- **Too many details.** Cut what does not serve the point.
- **Using a story in place of evidence.** One case is not a trend.
- **Inventing facts.** It damages trust.

## Practice

1. Name the components of a story.
2. What is narrative transportation?
3. What did Green and Brock (2000) find about highly transported readers?

### Answers

1. For example character, goal, obstacle, stakes, turning point and resolution.
2. Absorption into a story, with imagery, emotion and focused attention.
3. They noticed fewer flaws in the story and held more story-consistent beliefs.
