# Negotiation

## Summary

Negotiation is a process in which two or more parties with different interests try to reach an agreement. The best-known approach is principled negotiation, from Fisher and Ury (1981): separate people from the problem, focus on interests and not positions, invent options for mutual gain and use objective criteria. The key concepts are the BATNA (best alternative to a negotiated agreement), the reservation point and the zone of possible agreement. Research shows that common biases, such as the fixed-pie assumption and anchoring, shape outcomes.

## Core concepts

### Definition

*Negotiation* is a decision-making process between interdependent parties with partly conflicting and partly shared interests. Two broad approaches:

| Approach | Idea | Typical result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distributive (positional) | Fixed amount, one side's gain is the other's loss | Haggling over price |
| Integrative (interest-based) | Look for options that serve both sides' interests | Larger total value |

Fisher and Ury (1981) propose four principles: separate the people from the problem, focus on interests and not on positions, invent options for mutual gain and insist on objective criteria.

### Terminology

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Position | What a party says it wants |
| Interest | Why it wants it |
| BATNA | Best alternative to a negotiated agreement: what you do if no deal is reached |
| WATNA | Worst alternative to a negotiated agreement |
| Reservation point | The worst deal you would accept, set from your BATNA |
| ZOPA | Zone of possible agreement: the overlap of both sides' acceptable ranges |

### Analogy

A negotiation is like two people dividing a meal. If they argue over the one cake, someone loses, and if they ask what each wants, one may want the icing and the other the sponge. The limit of the analogy is that in real negotiations the sides do not always disclose their interests.

### Biases in negotiation

| Bias | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Fixed-pie assumption | Assuming that the other side's interests are opposed to yours on every issue (Thompson and Hastie, 1990) |
| Anchoring | The first offer pulls the final result toward it (Galinsky and Mussweiler, 2001) |
| Overconfidence | Overestimating one's chance of winning |
| Framing | Losses loom larger than gains (see [loss aversion](../../../../social-sciences/psychology/cognitive-psychology/loss-aversion.md)) |
| Escalation of commitment | Continuing because of past investment (see [sunk cost fallacy](../../../../social-sciences/psychology/cognitive-psychology/sunk-cost-fallacy.md)) |
| Reciprocity | Concessions create pressure to return concessions |

### Evidence

Thompson and Hastie (1990) found that many negotiators assume that the other side's interests are opposed to their own, and so miss trades. Galinsky and Mussweiler (2001) found that first offers anchor outcomes, and that taking the counterpart's perspective reduces the effect of an anchor.

### Tactics

- Prepare: know your interests, BATNA and limits, and estimate those of the other side.
- Consider making the first offer, when you are well informed, to anchor.
- Ask "why" questions to discover interests.
- Trade across issues: concede what matters less to you.
- Use objective criteria: market prices, standards, precedents.
- Keep the relationship in view when it continues.

Ethical standards: do not lie about facts, do not use threats you will not carry out, and respect confidentiality. Bluffing about your reservation point is common and carries risk to trust.

## Worked example

A freelancer and a client negotiate a project.

| Party | Position | Interest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Freelancer | 6,000 | Steady income, portfolio piece |
| Client | 4,000 | Stay within budget, deliver by June |

The positions show a gap of 2,000. Exploring interests produces an option: the client pays 5,000, the freelancer gets a testimonial and the right to show the work, and the delivery is split into two stages to ease the client's budget. Each side's BATNA: the freelancer has another small job at 3,500 for the same time, and the client has a quote of 5,500 from another provider. The ZOPA is between 3,500 and 5,500, so 5,000 is a deal for both.

## Trade offs and when to use it

Integrative bargaining takes more time and needs some trust. In a one-time price deal, the distributive approach is more common. Cultural and power differences change what is acceptable. Avoid agreeing just to end the discussion.

## Common mistakes

- **Negotiating positions only.** Ask about interests.
- **Not knowing your BATNA.** You then cannot judge an offer.
- **Assuming a fixed pie.** Look for trades.
- **Taking the first concession as weakness.** It builds reciprocity.

## Practice

1. What is a BATNA?
2. What is the difference between a position and an interest?
3. How does the fixed-pie assumption harm a negotiation?

### Answers

1. The best alternative to a negotiated agreement: what you do if no deal is reached.
2. A position is what a party says it wants, and an interest is the reason behind it.
3. It makes negotiators miss trades that serve both sides, since they assume the interests are opposed.

## Further reading

- Fisher, R. and Ury, W. (1981). *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In*. Houghton Mifflin. The source of principled negotiation, interests versus positions and BATNA.
- Thompson, L. and Hastie, R. (1990). Social perception in negotiation. *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*, 47(1), 98-123. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-5978(90)90048-E). Shows the fixed-pie assumption in negotiation.
- Galinsky, A. D. and Mussweiler, T. (2001). First offers as anchors: The role of perspective-taking and negotiator focus. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 81(4), 657-669. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.81.4.657). Shows that first offers anchor negotiation outcomes.
- Thompson, L. L. (2005). *The Mind and Heart of the Negotiator (3rd ed.)*. Pearson. A research-based text on negotiation, including bias and the fixed-pie assumption.
