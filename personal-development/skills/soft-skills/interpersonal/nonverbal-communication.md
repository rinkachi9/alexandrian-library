# Nonverbal communication and body language

## Summary

Nonverbal communication is everything people convey without words: facial expression, posture, gesture, gaze, touch, voice tone and use of space. It shows emotion and attitude and shapes first impressions, and it is easy to misread. The often quoted "7-38-55" rule, which says that only 7% of a message is in the words, is a misreading of two small experiments on single words. Cues to lying are weak and unreliable, and the claim that standing in a power pose changes hormones and behavior did not replicate.

## Core concepts

### Definition

*Nonverbal communication* is the transmission of meaning through signals other than words. Main channels:

| Channel | Examples | What it conveys |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Face | Smile, raised brows, tight lips | Emotion, interest |
| Gaze | Eye contact, looking away | Attention, intimacy, dominance, discomfort |
| Posture | Open or closed, leaning in | Engagement, confidence, comfort |
| Gesture | Hand movements, nods | Emphasis, agreement |
| Voice (paralanguage) | Pitch, pace, volume | Emotion, certainty |
| Space and touch | Distance, handshake | Relationship, culture |

### The 7-38-55 myth

Mehrabian and Wiener (1967) studied how people infer attitude when the words and the tone of voice of a single spoken word conflict, and a second study did the same for facial expression. The results were turned into the claim that 7% of communication is words, 38% tone and 55% body language. The authors' conditions were narrow: single words, and the feelings of the speaker. The rule does not apply to communication in general, and Mehrabian himself has said so.

### Analogy

Nonverbal signals are like punctuation and tone in a text: they change how the words are read, and are hard to interpret without the words. The limit of the analogy is that people also produce nonverbal signals without intending to.

### Reading signals

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  S[Observe a signal] --> B[Compare with the person's baseline]
  B --> C[Consider the context and culture]
  C --> H[Form a hypothesis]
  H --> T[Test: ask or watch more]
```

Reading works best when several signals (a cluster) agree, when the person's normal behavior is known and when the context is taken into account. A single sign, such as crossed arms, may mean cold or comfort. Elfenbein and Ambady (2002) found in a meta-analysis that people recognize emotions from facial expressions better than chance across cultures, and more accurately for members of their own cultural group, so culture matters.

### Eye contact

Kleinke (1986) reviewed research on gaze: eye contact is linked to perceived attention, warmth and credibility, and too much or too little is read as aggression or avoidance. Norms vary by culture and relationship, and a moderate, comfortable amount is typical in many Western settings.

### Detecting lies

DePaulo and colleagues (2003) analyzed 158 cues to deception and found that most had no reliable link to lying, and the strongest were small: liars tended to seem a little more tense and gave fewer details. Bond and DePaulo (2006) found that people judge lies and truths correctly 54% of the time. Common beliefs, such as that liars avoid eye contact, are not supported.

### Posture and confidence

Carney, Cuddy and Yap (2010) reported that holding expansive "power poses" for a minute raised testosterone, lowered cortisol and increased risk taking. A larger replication by Ranehill and colleagues (2015) found no effect on hormones or behavior, though participants reported feeling slightly more powerful. The hormone claims are not supported. Posture can still affect how one feels and appears: standing upright and open is a reasonable habit.

## Worked example

A manager in a meeting sees a colleague with crossed arms and short answers after a change is announced.

| Step | Reasoning |
| --- | --- |
| Signal | Crossed arms, few words |
| Baseline | The colleague is usually talkative |
| Context | A reorganization was just announced |
| Hypothesis | Worry or disagreement, not necessarily hostility |
| Test | Ask in private: "How do you see the change?" |

The manager treats the posture as a prompt to ask, not as proof.

## Trade offs and when to use it

Attention to nonverbal signals improves rapport and empathy. Over-reading leads to false conclusions, in particular about lying. Match the nonverbal signal to the words: mixed signals reduce trust.

## Common mistakes

- **Quoting 7-38-55.** It does not apply to general communication.
- **Reading one gesture.** Look for clusters and baseline.
- **Using body language to catch liars.** The accuracy is little above chance.
- **Believing power posing changes hormones.** Replication failed.

## Practice

1. What did the 7-38-55 studies actually examine?
2. How accurate are people at judging lies?
3. What did Ranehill and colleagues (2015) find?

### Answers

1. Inference of feelings from conflicting words and tone of voice for a single word, and from facial expression.
2. About 54% correct on average.
3. No effect of power posing on hormones or behavior, with slightly higher reported feelings of power.

- Kleinke, C. L. (1986). Gaze and eye contact: A research review. *Psychological Bulletin*, 100(1), 78-100. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.100.1.78). A review of the effects of gaze.
- Elfenbein, H. A. and Ambady, N. (2002). On the universality and cultural specificity of emotion recognition: A meta-analysis. *Psychological Bulletin*, 128(2), 203-235. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.128.2.203). A meta-analysis of emotion recognition across cultures.
- DePaulo, B. M., Lindsay, J. J., Malone, B. E. et al. (2003). Cues to deception. *Psychological Bulletin*, 129(1), 74-118. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.129.1.74). A meta-analysis of cues to deception.
- Bond, C. F. and DePaulo, B. M. (2006). Accuracy of deception judgments. *Personality and Social Psychology Review*, 10(3), 214-234. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327957pspr1003_2). A meta-analysis of 206 documents: people judge lies and truths correctly 54 percent of the time.
- Carney, D. R., Cuddy, A. J. and Yap, A. J. (2010). Power Posing. *Psychological Science*, 21(10), 1363-1368. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610383437). The original power posing study.
- Ranehill, E., Dreber, A., Johannesson, M. et al. (2015). Assessing the Robustness of Power Posing. *Psychological Science*, 26(5), 653-656. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614553946). A larger replication that did not find hormonal or behavioral effects of power posing.
