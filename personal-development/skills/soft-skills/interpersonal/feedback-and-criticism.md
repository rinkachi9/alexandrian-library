# Feedback, criticism and assertiveness

## Summary

Feedback is information about performance that is given to help improve it. A major meta-analysis found that feedback improves performance on average, and that more than a third of feedback interventions made performance worse, which depends on whether the feedback directs attention to the task or to the self. Giving constructive criticism well means being specific, timely and focused on behavior. Receiving it well means managing the emotional triggers that make feedback hard to hear. Assertiveness, speaking up respectfully for one's needs, supports both.

## Core concepts

### Definition

*Feedback* is information about the gap between actual and desired performance. *Constructive criticism* is feedback that aims to improve and identifies specific changes. *Destructive criticism* attacks the person or gives no way forward. Kluger and DeNisi (1996) reviewed 607 effects of feedback interventions on performance. Feedback raised performance on average, but in more than one third of the cases it lowered it. Feedback lowered performance most when it drew attention to the self (praise or blame of the person) and raised it most when it focused on the task and how to improve it.

### Analogy

Feedback is like a navigation instruction: "turn left in 200 meters" helps, and "you are a bad driver" does not. The limit of the analogy is that a driver does not feel judged by the device.

### Constructive and destructive criticism

| Aspect | Constructive | Destructive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Focus | Specific behavior and its effect | The person's character |
| Timing | Soon after, in private | Late or in public |
| Direction | What to do next | Only what was wrong |
| Tone | Respectful, factual | Sarcastic or angry |
| Example | "In the meeting, you talked over Sam twice. Let him finish, then add your point." | "You are rude." |

A common structure is situation, behavior, impact, request: "In the Tuesday review (situation), you presented without the chart (behavior), so the group could not follow the numbers (impact). Next time, can you bring the chart (request)?"

### Receiving feedback

Stone and Heen (2014) describe three triggers that make feedback hard to hear: *truth* ("this is wrong or unfair"), *relationship* ("who are you to say this?") and *identity* ("this threatens who I am"). Strategies:

| Step | Action |
| --- | --- |
| Pause | Do not respond at once |
| Ask | Ask for examples, and what "better" looks like |
| Separate | Distinguish the content from the delivery |
| Decide | Take what is useful, and decline what is not |
| Thank | Show that feedback is welcome, so people give more |

Reject feedback when it is clearly wrong, comes from a source with no information, or is a personal attack. System factors also matter: if many people struggle with the same thing, the cause may be the process and not the individual.

### Assertiveness

*Assertiveness* is expressing one's needs, opinions and boundaries directly and respectfully (Alberti and Emmons, 1970). It is distinguished from other styles.

| Style | Behavior | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Passive | Avoids conflict, does not say what is needed | Resentment, needs unmet |
| Aggressive | Wins at the cost of others | Damaged relationships |
| Passive-aggressive | Expresses anger indirectly | Confusion, distrust |
| Assertive | States needs clearly, respects others | Clear, respectful |

### Difficult people

With rude or hostile people, keep to a few steps: stay calm and slow the pace, acknowledge the feeling without agreeing with the behavior ("I can see you are upset"), restate the issue and the next step, set a limit ("I will continue when we can talk calmly"), and end the conversation if needed. Ury (2007) describes similar steps for negotiating with a difficult counterpart: do not react, step to their side, reframe, build a bridge and use power to educate, not to escalate.

## Worked example

A colleague interrupts you repeatedly in meetings.

| Style | Response |
| --- | --- |
| Passive | Say nothing and fume |
| Aggressive | "You never let anyone speak!" |
| Assertive | In private: "In the last two meetings, I was interrupted several times when I was making my point. I would like to finish before you respond. Can we agree on that?" |

The assertive version names the behavior and the impact, makes a request and leaves room for the colleague to answer.

## Trade offs and when to use it

Direct feedback works best in a relationship with some trust and a shared goal. In hierarchical or cross-cultural settings, directness is read differently, so adapt the form and keep the content honest. Assertiveness has limits in unsafe situations, in which leaving is the better step.

## Common mistakes

- **Attacking the person.** Describe behavior and impact.
- **Sandwiching so that the message is lost.** Be clear about the main point.
- **Reacting at once to feedback.** Pause and ask.
- **Confusing assertiveness with aggression.** Respect is part of it.

## Further reading

- Kluger, A. N. and DeNisi, A. (1996). The effects of feedback interventions on performance: A historical review, a meta-analysis, and a preliminary feedback intervention theory. *Psychological Bulletin*, 119(2), 254-284. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254). A meta-analysis of feedback interventions that finds that about a third reduce performance.

- Alberti, R. E. and Emmons, M. L. (1970). *Your Perfect Right: A Guide to Assertive Living*. Impact Publishers. An early practical guide that separates assertive, passive and aggressive behavior.
- Ury, W. (2007). *Getting Past No: Negotiating in Difficult Situations*. Bantam. Practical steps for negotiating with difficult counterparts.
