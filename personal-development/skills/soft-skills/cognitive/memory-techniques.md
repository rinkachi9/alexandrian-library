# Memory and memorization techniques

## Summary

Human working memory holds only a handful of items at once, about four meaningful chunks according to modern estimates, which replaced Miller's classic "seven plus or minus two". Long-term memory is much larger, and what matters is how information is encoded and retrieved. Techniques that work include chunking, mnemonic systems such as the method of loci, and, for learning in general, retrieval practice and spacing. Memory champions use these methods and do not have unusual brains.

## Core concepts

### Definition

*Working memory* is the limited-capacity system that holds and manipulates information for a short time. Miller (1956) described a span of about seven items, and Cowan (2001) argued that the real capacity is about four chunks when rehearsal and grouping are controlled. A *chunk* is a unit that the person treats as one item, such as a familiar word or date. *Mnemonics* are devices that encode information in a form that is easier to retrieve.

### Analogy

Working memory is the desk and long-term memory is the archive. The desk is small, so what matters is how well the files are labeled and filed. The limit of the analogy is that memory is reconstructed at retrieval, and not read out like a file.

### Techniques

| Technique | How it works | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chunking | Group items into meaningful units | Phone numbers, codes |
| Method of loci | Place items along a familiar route and walk it mentally | Ordered lists, speeches |
| Keyword and imagery | Link a new word to a vivid image | Vocabulary |
| Acronyms and rhymes | Compress items into a cue | Short lists |
| Retrieval practice | Test yourself instead of rereading | Any study material |
| Spaced repetition | Revisit at growing intervals | Long-term retention |

Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated learning techniques by evidence. Practice testing and distributed practice received high utility, and highlighting and rereading received low. The keyword mnemonic and imagery help in some tasks and have limited reach.

### Memory champions

Maguire and colleagues (2003) studied top memorizers and found no differences in brain structure and intelligence from controls. The champions used the method of loci, and showed more activation of regions for spatial memory. Dresler and colleagues (2017) trained novices in the method of loci for six weeks and found improved recall and changes in brain network connectivity. Superior memory is a trained skill.

```mermaid
  R --> S
```

### Offloading

Not everything needs to be memorized: notes, lists and calendars free working memory for thinking. Write down what matters and review it on a schedule.

## Worked example

Memorizing a shopping list of six items with the method of loci.

| Place on the route | Item | Image |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Front door | Eggs | A door dripping with raw eggs |
| Hallway | Bread | A giant loaf as a hall carpet |
| Kitchen sink | Milk | The tap pours milk |
| Living-room sofa | Apples | Apples as sofa cushions |
| Bedroom | Rice | Rice scattered on the bed |
| Bathroom | Soap | A tower of soap in the bath |

Walking the route in the mind retrieves the items in order. The more unusual the image, the better. After a day, a second walk-through strengthens the trace.

## Trade offs and when to use it

Mnemonics are good for arbitrary, ordered or detail-heavy material. For understanding, meaning and structure are better than tricks. Spaced retrieval practice is the best general method for learning that has to last.

## Common mistakes

- **Rereading as the main method.** It feels fluent and teaches less.
- **Memorizing without understanding.** Understanding makes recall easier.
- **Cramming.** Spacing gives lasting retention.

## Practice

1. What capacity did Cowan (2001) propose for working memory?
2. What did Maguire and colleagues (2003) find about memory champions?
3. Name two techniques with high utility in Dunlosky and colleagues (2013).

### Answers

1. About four chunks.
2. Their brains and intelligence did not differ from controls, and they used mnemonic strategies such as the method of loci.
3. Practice testing and distributed practice.

## Further reading

- Miller, G. A. (1956). The magical number seven, plus or minus two: Some limits on our capacity for processing information. *Psychological Review*, 63(2), 81-97. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0043158). The classic paper on the limits of short-term memory.
- Cowan, N. (2001). The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of mental storage capacity. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 24(1), 87-114. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X01003922). Argues that working memory holds about four chunks.
- Maguire, E. A., Valentine, E. R., Wilding, J. M. et al. (2002). Routes to remembering: the brains behind superior memory. *Nature Neuroscience*, 6(1), 90-95. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn988). Finds that memory champions use the method of loci and not special brains.
- Dresler, M., Shirer, W. R., Konrad, B. N. et al. (2017). Mnemonic Training Reshapes Brain Networks to Support Superior Memory. *Neuron*, 93(5), 1227-1235.e6. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2017.02.003). Shows that mnemonic training changes brain networks.
- Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J. et al. (2013). Improving Students’ Learning With Effective Learning Techniques. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 14(1), 4-58. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266). Rates ten learning techniques by evidence.
